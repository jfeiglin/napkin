# AI uplift for signals-intelligence collection and processing: machine translation, speech-to-text, triage, and the share of take that is processed

These notes test the SC45 inputs for its collection specialists. SC45 covers one country's cyber attacks and surveillance, 2026–2051. Research date: 2026-11-08.

**SC45 inputs that these notes test.** The codes appear under "Maps to" in each finding.
- **S1 Workforce and split.** About 32,000 collection specialists. Half run collection on implants and half process the take.
- **S2 Exploited share.** The specialists fully exploit about 12% of the implants held (2011: 8,448 of 68,975 machines).
- **S3 Automation of implant work.** AI and automation do 30% of the work of running implants today and 90% at the end of AI progress.
- **S4 AI share of processing.** AI does 10% of processing (translation, triage) today and 60% at the end, on a slower path.
- **S5 Wait before processing.** The take waits about 0.1 year (about 5 weeks) before processing. The scenario has no source for this value.
- **S6 Share never processed.** SC45 may have no such input. The findings inform S2 and S5.

## 1. What share of collected SIGINT and cyber-espionage take is processed, and how large are backlogs and delays?

### Takeaway
No official source gives the share of implant (CNE) take that is processed. Bulk collection shows a very steep funnel: the NSA said in 2013 that its analysts look at 0.00004% of world internet traffic, and a whole-country voice program let humans hear "only a fraction of 1 percent" of recorded calls. Targeted collection is different: FBI audits (2003–2005) found 71–73% of all foreign-language audio reviewed and 93% of counterterrorism audio reviewed; the counterterrorism backlog equals about 2–11 weeks of intake, and low-priority take was about one third unreviewed and partly deleted. Bulk buffers keep content for 3 to 30 days, which caps the wait for bulk take; an NSA official said in 2026 that current methods "can take days or weeks".

### Inferences
- **Two different funnels.** Bulk passive collection shows human review shares from about 10⁻⁷ to below 10⁻² (NSA 2013; MYSTIC). Targeted collection on chosen lines shows review shares of about 70–99% (FBI). Implant take in SC45 is targeted take, so the FBI series is the closer transfer for S6; the bulk figures are a lower bound only.
- **Marginal unreviewed share, FBI, Dec 2003 to Mar 2005 (15 months).** New audio: 2,589,918 − 1,676,787 = 913,131 hours. New unreviewed: 707,742 − 478,573 = 229,169 hours. So about 25% of new audio stayed unreviewed (CT about 6%, CI about 31%). This agrees with the 2009 audit (about 25% of audio). Maps to: S6 for a well-funded force without AI, about 6% for counterterrorism take and about 30% for counterintelligence take. The highest-priority cases had no backlog.
- **Backlog expressed in years of intake (my arithmetic, FY2004 intake).** All CT audio: 38,514 ÷ 182,014 ≈ 0.21 year (about 11 weeks). Refined CT backlog: 8,354 ÷ 182,014 ≈ 0.046 year (about 2.4 weeks). CI: 669,228 ÷ 579,595 ≈ 1.15 years. All audio: 707,742 ÷ 761,609 ≈ 0.93 year. Under a steady queue with first-in, first-out order (Little's law), the mean wait equals the backlog divided by the intake rate. Real queues are priority-ordered and some take is deleted, so the wait for take that analysts do process is shorter than these values.
- **S5 reading.** The scenario's 0.1 year sits between the FBI's priority CT backlog (about 0.05 year) and its whole CT backlog (about 0.2 year). It is also the upper end of the NSA's 2026 "days or weeks". For bulk take, the 3-day and 30-day buffers cap the wait at about 0.008–0.08 year. A range of about 0.01–0.25 year for targeted take fits this evidence; the 0.1-year value can now cite the FBI series and the 2026 NSA remark.
- **Human capacity is language-bound, not only headcount-bound.** At the FBI standard of 1,000 audio hours per linguist-year (see section 2), FY2004 audio needed about 762 linguist-years and text about 60 (2,980,462 pages ÷ 50,000). That is about 820 linguist-years against 1,338 linguists in March 2005, yet the backlog grew. Many contract linguists may work part time, and the FBI met its staffing target in only 23 of 52 languages. So capacity in the right language is the binding limit. Machine translation relieves this limit more than it raises total throughput.
- **SIGINT system residence time.** 2 petabytes in the system against an expected 1 terabyte per second gives about 2,000 seconds (about 33 minutes) of inflow. This rough figure says that raw bulk data is filtered or dropped soon after arrival. It does not describe stored targeted take.
- **Triage yield.** The GCHQ example (10 interesting leads in 300) is a yield of about 3%. It is an illustration, not a measurement.

### Gaps
- I found no official or reported share of implant (CNE) take that is processed or analyzed. The 2011 GENIE figure (section 4) measures implants fully used, not take processed.
- I found no backlog or delay figure for the NSA, GCHQ or any other SIGINT agency after 2005. The FBI audits are the only official series, and they end in FY2008.
- PCLOB and IPCO reports, in the parts I could see, give no share of collected material that analysts review. The ISC redacted its numbers.

## 2. Machine translation and speech-to-text in intelligence: adoption, accuracy, share of work by machine, productivity per linguist

### Takeaway
The NSA has run speech-to-text since at least 1999 and machine translation since at least 2008 in published documents; in 2024 it still uses machine translation to triage, with human linguists who check the output. No agency publishes the share of translation work that machines do. Transfer studies give productivity multiples per translator of about 1.2–1.7 for statistical and neural machine translation. My rough conversion of a large commercial edit-time series gives about 3.6 by 2022–2023 for high-resource text. Speech recognition helps human transcribers only when the word error rate is below about 30%; accented, tonal, noisy and low-resource speech remains weak. The FBI planning standard gives a human baseline: one linguist reviews about 1,000 audio hours or 50,000 pages a year.

### Inferences
- **From productivity multiple to the AI share of the work.** If AI raises output per linguist by a factor m, the AI share of the work is 1 − 1/m. Phrase-based MT in literary text (m = 1.18) gives about 15%. NMT in literary text (m = 1.36) gives about 26%. Statistical MT in localization (m = 1.74) gives about 43%.
- **From edit time to a multiple (rough).** A from-scratch rate of 503 words per hour is about 7.2 seconds per word. An edit time of 3 seconds gives m ≈ 2.4 (about 58%); 2 seconds gives m ≈ 3.6 (about 72%); 1 second gives m ≈ 7.2 (about 86%). This mixes a literary study with a commercial workflow, so treat it as an order of magnitude for high-resource text only.
- **Volume share is not labor share.** Machines indexed nearly all NSA voice cuts by 2006, but human hours still did most of the processing work. S4 should stay a labor share.
- **S4 today.** For high-resource text, the transfer studies support an AI labor share of about 25–45% even before LLMs. Noisy speech, low-resource languages, triage judgment and report writing pull the average down. The scenario's 10% for all processing is plausible but at the low end; about 10–30% for 2026 fits the evidence.
- **Slower path for processing.** Speech accuracy limits (the 30% crossover, accent gaps, tonal languages) and data limits for low-resource languages support a slower path for processing than for routine implant work.
- **Language-bound capacity.** The FBI example (section 1) suggests that AI translation adds most value where no human linguist exists for a language. A scenario that treats processing capacity as one pool may understate this effect.

### Gaps
- No agency publishes the share of translation or transcription that machines do, or a productivity multiple per intelligence linguist.
- I found no public word error rate for intercepted speech, and no public machine-translation quality figure for intelligence languages.
- I found no current (2024–2026) count of US or UK SIGINT linguists, and no official plan that replaces linguists with AI.
- The NSA 2024 podcast transcript was not readable (HTTP 403); its wording is from a search summary.
- I did not check the academic papers beyond search summaries. The Läubli baseline and the Gaur 45% value are unverified.

## 3. LLM triage and summarization of collected data: official statements, throughput, time savings

### Takeaway
LLM adoption in intelligence is broad: more than 7,000 NSA analysts used generative AI by mid-2024, and the CIA's Osiris tool serves thousands of analysts across 18 agencies. No agency has published a time saving or a share of work. The only quantified case of a state actor is a 2025 Chinese state-sponsored campaign: per Anthropic, AI did 80–90% of the campaign, including the sorting of stolen data by intelligence value, but it also made up findings. ## 4. Automation of running implants at scale: TURBINE and public figures on implants per operator

### Takeaway
In 2011 GENIE's 1,870 staff held 68,975 implants (about 37 per person) but fully used 8,448 (12.2%, about 4.5 per person); the Post says that human operators were the limit. NSA documents (2009) say that "Human 'drivers' limit ability for large-scale exploitation", and TURBINE (in use since at least July 2010) controls implants "by groups instead of individually", with a goal of "potentially millions". An undated document says that the NSA managed an average of about 1,543 active implants. In 2025 an AI agent framework did 80–90% of a state espionage campaign, with 4–6 human decisions per campaign.

### Cited Findings

- **Implants per person, 2011.** 68,975 ÷ 1,870 ≈ 37 implants held per GENIE staff member; 8,448 ÷ 1,870 ≈ 4.5 fully used per staff member.
