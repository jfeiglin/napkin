## 1. What probability-of-damage, confidence and timing standards do US targeting and fires use, and what do public doctrine and practice say about cyber effects in plans?

### Takeaway
Public US planning numbers are per-target assurance levels, not tolerances on the share of targets hit. Nuclear war plans of 1960–63 used 70–97% assurance of severe damage, and 1941 bomber planners sized the force for a 90% chance to destroy a target. Planners reached these levels by assigning more weapons to each target (2.4–3 nuclear weapons per target; about 220 bombers per target). Tactical fires doctrine sets timing in minutes around a fixed time on target, with a fallback if the enabling effect is late. Cyber doctrine (JP 3-12, 2018) asks that capabilities "reliably create the intended effects" and normally names start and stop times, but gives no numbers. A Cyber JMEM exists (first increment accredited in FY2020). In 2026 the Joint Chiefs chairman called Cyber Command a "first mover" in the Iran strikes, which shows that US plans now count on opening cyber effects, but no public reliability data exists.

### Cited Findings

**A. Kinetic weaponeering and assurance levels**

- **Definition of probability of damage (Pd), with illustrative values.** "Pd is used to express the statistical probability that a specified damage criteria can be met assuming the probability of arrival." The example compares "a Pd of .70 against an aircraft parking area containing 10 aircraft" with "a Pd of .40 against an area containing 30 aircraft": "only seven aircraft would be killed in the first target area, while 12 would be killed in the second". JMEM outputs include "numbers of sorties or passes required to achieve specified damage levels in terms of stated damage criteria". Weapon reliability is a separate factor: "Warhead functioning dependability". — [USAF Intelligence Targeting Guide, AFPAM 14-210, chapter 6](https://irp.fas.org/doddir/usaf/afpam14-210/part06.htm). Date: 1 February 1998 (older). Read: chapter 6, fetch extract. Kind: doctrine definition and an illustrative example, not a measured value. Applies: transfer. → acceptance: gives no standard confidence level. It shows that Pd works as an expected fraction, and that planners count arrival and weapon reliability as separate stages.

- **Nuclear war plan assurance levels, SIOP-62 (1961).** A 1961 CIA document describes "targets with an assurance of 70 percent or greater of receiving severe damage, considering all factors of attrition and reliability except pre-launch destruction of SIOP forces", and says "the SIOP-62 stipulated a 95 percent assurance factor for the destruction of targets 'representing a direct threat'" to the continental US. — [CIA reading room, 1961-06-30a](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/1961-06-30a.pdf). Date: 1961; the file name suggests 30 June 1961 (older). Read: search-engine summary only. Kind: planning standard. Applies: transfer. → acceptance: supports a range of 0.70–0.95.

- **Delivery assurance and SIOP-63.** A National Security Archive summary reports that SIOP-62 "assurance of delivery averaged 85 percent, greater than the 75 percent minimum set by the U.S. high command". Another National Security Archive summary says SIOP-63 "established a high damage expectancy of 90 percent probability of severe damage to targets". — [National Security Archive, "New Evidence on the Origins of Overkill"](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb236/index.htm) or [National Security Archive node 2259](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/node/2259). The search summary did not say which of these two pages carries each figure. Date: 2009–2011 publication of 1961–63 records (older). Read: search-engine summary only. Kind: planning standard from official records. Applies: transfer. → acceptance: supports 0.75–0.90.

- **SAC set a higher level than the guidance.** In a 1986 interview, George Rathjens said that the guidance for the level of destruction was set at around 90 percent, that SAC took this as a minimum, and that SAC set its own levels at 97 percent (paraphrase in a search summary; I could not confirm the exact words). — [WGBH Open Vault, War and Peace in the Nuclear Age, interview with George Rathjens](https://openvault.wgbh.org/catalog/V_9A93249305BB4FBC9CE4F8F6F52CCB56). Date: 1986 (older). Read: search-engine summary only. Kind: a participant's recollection. Applies: transfer. → acceptance: the operational command went above 0.95. This is a reason to keep the upper end of the range at 0.95 or higher.

- **Weapons per target under the SIOP-62 damage criteria.** "it would take three 80 kiloton weapons to destroy a city like Nagasaki", according to "the damage expectancy criteria of SIOP-62". The plan options "would have delivered 1706 nuclear weapons against a total of 725 targets", and the preemptive option delivered "over 3200 nuclear weapons to 1060 targets". — [National Security Archive, Electronic Briefing Book 130, press release](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB130/press.htm). Date: 13 July 2004 (older). Read: page in full, fetch extract. Kind: historical record. Applies: transfer. My calculation: 2.4 and 3.0 weapons per target. → tolerance: planners bought assurance with redundancy, not with a forecast of the share of targets hit.

- **Criticism of the damage criteria.** In 1960, science adviser George Kistiakowsky asked whether the damage criteria "result in overkill and will create unjustified additional 'force requirements'". — [Foreign Relations of the United States 1958–60, vol. III, document 127](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v03/d127). Date: 1960 (older). Read: search-engine summary only; I did not confirm that this page holds the quote. Kind: opinion of an official. Applies: transfer. → acceptance: very high levels have a cost in force size, which limits how high planners go.

- **Bomber planning in 1941 (AWPD-1) used a 90% target.** "According to data from training and practice bombing, a heavy bomber at 20,000 feet had a 1.2 percent probability of hitting a 100-foot-square target. About 220 bombers would be required for 90 percent probability of destroying the target. AWPD-1 forecast a need for 251 combat groups to carry out the plan." — [John T. Correll, "Daylight Precision Bombing", Air Force Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/2008/October%202008/1008daylight.pdf). Date: October 2008, about 1940–41 planning (older). Read: full article text. Kind: historical planning factor that a historian reports. Applies: transfer and analogy. My check: with independent bombs, 1 − (1 − 0.012)^n ≥ 0.9 needs n ≈ 191. The planners' 220 is about 15% above that value. → acceptance: supports 0.9 as a planning target. → success per shot: this doctrine was planned at 1.2% per bomb.

- **Desert Storm audit (GAO, 1996–97).** "only 8 percent of the delivered munitions tonnage was guided, but at a price that represented 84 percent of the total munitions cost". "the claimed one-target, one-bomb capability for laser-guided munitions was not demonstrated in the air campaign where, on average, 11 tons of guided and 44 tons of unguided munitions were delivered on each successfully destroyed target." The earlier report found that "the success rate of guided munitions could not be validated by campaign data". — [GAO/NSIAD-97-134 summary, GlobalSecurity copy](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/report/gao/nsiad97134/summary.htm); [GAO/PEMD-96-10](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-PEMD-96-10/html/GAOREPORTS-PEMD-96-10.htm). Date: June 1997 and July 1996 (older). Read: search-engine summary only; the GlobalSecurity copy refused the fetch (HTTP 403). Kind: audit measurement. Applies: transfer. A trade magazine answered that "on average, 2.2 precision guided munitions were expended per target destroyed" ([Air & Space Forces Magazine, September 1996 editorial](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/MagazineArchive/Documents/1996/September%201996/0996edit.pdf); opinion; search summary). → tolerance: the planned efficiency and the delivered efficiency differed by large factors. → noisy assessment: the auditors could not validate success rates from campaign data.

**B. Timing tolerance in fires**

- **Time on target (TOT) and the window for suppression of enemy air defenses (SEAD).** "TOT is a time at which the aircraft bombs are to impact the target and around which supporting surface fires can be coordinated." "GPS time is the standard for US and allied forces in establishing a common time reference and for setting TOT. Strict adherence to timing by participants is required for aircraft safety." A sample brief: "TOT 1410, SEAD minus 2 through minus 1 and plus 1 through plus 2." "if SEAD is required, the JTAC must know the SEAD timeline so that he can shift the CAS TOT if the SEAD is late." "Mark on target 30-45 seconds before time on target/time to target." — [JP 3-09.3, Close Air Support](https://irp.fas.org/doddir/dod/jp3_09_3.pdf). Date: 25 November 2014 (older; a 2019 edition replaces it, and I did not read that edition). Read: relevant sections of the primary PDF. Kind: doctrine. Applies: transfer. → campaign_time and tolerance: an enabling effect at a set time must hold within about 1–2 minutes, and the plan has a fallback (shift the attack) if it is late. The model's test has no timing term.

**C. Cyber doctrine and cyber weaponeering**

- **Industry opinion on confidence in cyber effects.** JMEMs are "essentially a guide with probabilities of success for the full range of conventional munitions against specific targets". ### Inferences
- The model's acceptance range (0.7–0.95) matches the span of historical US per-target assurance levels: 0.70 minimum, 0.75 delivery minimum, 0.85 average, 0.90 for SIOP-63 and AWPD-1, 0.95 for top-priority targets, and 0.97 at SAC. These are per-target probabilities under an assumption of independent failures. Planners reached them with 2–3 weapons per target. The model's test (the share of targets hit within a tolerance) is a different quantity, so this support is an analogy.
- Tactical doctrine holds enabling effects to about ±1–2 minutes around a fixed time on target and plans a fallback. JP 3-12 asks for start and stop times. A one-month test of the share of targets hit, with no timing term, is much looser than the standard for "a network down at a set hour". The model could treat a scheduled effect as two events, the effect and its timing, each with its own chance.
- The 2026 statements show that US plans already count on opening cyber effects ("first movers", "before the attack began"). For opening effects, doctrine in the model's sense may already exist in 2026. For effects that must hold for a whole campaign, there is no public evidence of doctrine.
- The Sandworm case gives one public measure of an effects chain: about 4 months from first access to effect, about 2 months to build the capability, and a ready capability held for about 3 weeks. This supports separate stages for access, capability, a wait for the set time, effect, and follow-on cover-up (the wiper two days later).

## 2. How did doctrine form for earlier weapons with low single-shot reliability but high volume, and what reliability or volume came before doctrine?

### Takeaway
Institutional reliance often formed at low single-shot reliability when the weapon was cheap and used in mass: Ukrainian forces built drone units across the front while 60–80% of FPV drones failed to reach the target, and drones still caused 60–70% of damaged and destroyed Russian systems. But this reliance is for attrition, not for scheduled effects. Strike timing depends on gaps in enemy jamming, and officers still want artillery for responsive fires. For precision weapons, a demonstrated single-shot hit rate of about 50% (1972) came two to three decades before guided weapons became most of the munitions used (8% in 1991, 35% in 1999, 68% in 2003). US bomber doctrine formed in the 1930s "on the basis of very thin evidence", before accuracy was proven, and wartime evidence then forced changes.

### Cited Findings

**Precision-guided munitions**

- **Laser-guided bombs (LGBs) in Southeast Asia, 1968–1973.** In 1968 combat tests, the Mk 84 version "averaged 20 feet of accuracy, and about one in four bombs scored a direct hit". "From February 1972 to February 1973, over 10,500 LGBs were dropped. About 5,100 were direct hits, and about 4,000 more landed within a 25-foot CEP." LGBs were used in about 10% of attacks on tanks but caused 22% of tanks destroyed. In 1972 an LGB cost about $4,000 and an electro-optical guided bomb about $17,000. After Vietnam, the Air Force "did not go all-out for laser-guided bombs". — [John T. Correll, "The Emergence of Smart Bombs", Air Force Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0310bombs/). Date: 1 March 2010 (older; data 1968–2003). Read: full, fetch extract. Kind: a historian's compilation of official figures. Applies: analogy. My calculation: 49% direct hits and 87% direct or within 25 feet. → success_best: a mature-weapon hit rate near 0.5–0.9, depending on the definition of a hit.

- **Thanh Hoa Bridge.** "By 1972, the Air Force and Navy had flown 871 sorties against the bridge and lost 11 aircraft without destroying it." On 13 May 1972, "F-4s dropped 26 LGBs on the bridge, and the western span was knocked off its abutment." — [Correll 2010](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0310bombs/) (as above). Conflicts: other accounts give 24 LGBs (nine 3,000-lb and fifteen 2,000-lb) plus 48 unguided bombs, the date 12 May with 16 aircraft, and "over 1,000 missions" across Rolling Thunder ([Wikipedia, Thanh Hóa Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanh_H%C3%B3a_Bridge); search summary). Kind: historical record. Applies: analogy. → a single demonstration changed planners' view of what was possible for one target type, but not the force-wide doctrine.

- **Share of guided munitions by campaign.** Desert Storm (1991): "8% of munitions dropped, compared with under 1% in Vietnam". Kosovo (1999): guided bombs were "35% of munitions used but 74% of targets destroyed"; B-2 JDAMs placed "90% of their weapons within 10 meters". Iraq (2003): "68% of munitions were guided, including 22.4% JDAMs and 29.5% LGBs". — [Correll 2010](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0310bombs/) (as above; fetch extract). Kind: official figures that a historian reports. Applies: analogy.

- **Iraq 2003 count.** "Operation Iraqi Freedom—By the Numbers" (USCENTAF, 30 April 2003) covers 19 March to 18 April 2003 and lists 29,199 munitions: 19,948 guided and 9,251 unguided. The guided munitions included 6,542 JDAMs. — [USCENTAF report, Commonwealth Institute copy](https://www.comw.org/pda/fulltext/oifcentaf.pdf); [GovExec](https://govexec.com/defense/2003/05/air-force-assesses-impact-of-iraq-campaign/14112). Date: 2003 (older). Read: search-engine summary only. Kind: official count. Applies: analogy. My calculation: 68.3% guided in a 30-day campaign. → campaign_time: one month is the length of the main 2003 air campaign.

- **Desert Storm audit.** See section 1 (GAO: 8% of tonnage, 84% of cost; 11 tons guided and 44 tons unguided per destroyed target).

**Strategic bombing between the wars and in World War II**

- **US doctrine formed before accuracy was proven.** "Daylight precision bombing became Air Force doctrine". The theory "developed in the 1930s at the Air Corps Tactical School at Maxwell Field". "on the basis of very thin evidence", the Air Corps concluded that the new bombers flew too high and too fast for fighters. In 1940, Gen. Arnold declared, "The Air Corps is committed to a strategy of high-altitude precision bombing of military objectives." — [Correll, "Daylight Precision Bombing", Air Force Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/2008/October%202008/1008daylight.pdf). Date: October 2008 (older). Read: full article text. Kind: history. Applies: analogy. → passes_needed: a counterexample. Doctrine came from belief, not from repeated passes.

- **Wartime accuracy and losses.** "The average circular error in 1943 was 1,200 feet, meaning that only 16 percent of the bombs fell within 1,000 feet of the aiming point." "As the loss rate spiked to eight percent in early 1943, crews calculated their chances of surviving a 25-mission combat tour." Losses were "30 percent" on the Ploesti mission (August 1943) and "28 percent" against Schweinfurt (October 1943). "By 1945, Eighth Air Force was operating at much lower altitudes and was putting up to 60 percent of its bombs within 1,000 feet of the aiming point". Postwar analysis: "Eighth Air Force in Great Britain put 31.8 percent of its bombs within 1,000 feet of the aim point from an average altitude of 21,000 feet." ## 3. What does the military innovation literature say about time from first use to doctrine, and is there a quantitative relationship?

### Takeaway
I found no quantitative law for the time from first use to doctrine. The classic works are qualitative. Rosen (1991) finds that peacetime innovation is long and depends on promotion paths for officers who learn the new way of war, and that wartime innovation is held back by poor intelligence and short time. Horowitz (2010) explains adoption by cost ("financial intensity") and the amount of organizational change ("organizational capital"). The cases in section 2 give a working range: about 1–2 years in war, and about 10–30 years in peace.

### Cited Findings

## 4. How do planners treat correlated failure, and how do they buy reliability with volume?

### Takeaway
Correlated failure is the normal case under countermeasures. Jamming, weather, interceptor drones, a design defect or one countermeasure can defeat a whole class of shots in an area or a period. Planners answer in four ways: they assign more weapons per target (2–3 in nuclear plans; about 220 bombers in 1941; 11 tons of guided and 44 tons of unguided munitions per destroyed target in 1991), they suppress the countermeasure first (SEAD, attacks on jammers, chaff), they combine different weapons (drones with artillery), and they adapt fast (fixes in weeks to months). Volume removes independent noise but not the common factor. In Russia's Shahed campaign, month-to-month changes in the hit share were about 5–6 times larger than independent shots would give, and the largest raids often had the lowest hit rates.

### Cited Findings
- **Jamming and weather act on whole periods.** "there are long periods where either EW or the weather significantly degrades UAV operations." "With FPVs that are remotely piloted by radio frequency, it is also difficult to concentrate multiple drones in time and space because they can interfere with one another's guidance systems." "EW is effective in significantly reducing the reliability of UAVs and disrupting the accurate fixing of targets to cue strikes. However, it is also causing widespread issues with deconfliction and fratricide causes friendly UAV operations to be periodic rather than continuous." — [RUSI, 14 February 2025](https://static.rusi.org/tactical-developments-third-year-russo-ukrainian-war-february-2205.pdf). Read: relevant sections of the primary PDF. Kind: authors' assessment from interviews. Applies: transfer. → the common factor in the model: one condition degrades all shots in a period, and salvo size has an upper limit.

- **Planners create windows by suppressing the countermeasure.** Strike gaps "can be induced through the suppression of enemy electronic warfare (EW) using artillery, or through planned pauses in friendly jamming to enable strike systems to get airborne." ### Inferences
- **My test of the model's doctrine rule on the Shahed data.** I used the 17 monthly hit shares from the ISIS Table 1 (January 2025 to May 2026). A planner who forecasts each month's hit share from the month before:

  | Tolerance on the hit share | Months within tolerance (forecast = last month) | Months within tolerance (forecast = mean of the last 3 months) | From April 2025 on (forecast = last month) |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | ±10% | 0 of 16 (0.00) | 3 of 14 (0.21) | 0 of 13 (0.00) |
  | ±25% | 10 of 16 (0.62) | 7 of 14 (0.50) | 9 of 13 (0.69) |
  | ±50% | 14 of 16 (0.88) | 12 of 14 (0.86) | 13 of 13 (1.00) |

  With the model's defaults (±25%, 80% chance), this mass campaign fails the test (0.50–0.69). With ±50% it passes (0.86–1.00). If the shots were independent, the binomial noise of a monthly share with 2,400–8,200 launches is only 3–10% (relative standard deviation). The observed standard deviation of the month-to-month log change is 0.41 (all months) and 0.24 (from April 2025). Independent shots alone would give 0.066 and 0.051. The common factor (defender adaptation, tactics, target choice, weather) is therefore about 5–6 times larger than the noise from independent shots. This is my calculation from reported counts; the script is not part of the project. Applies: transfer from a drone campaign to cyber attacks.
- **What this means for the "casino" argument.** The casino logic holds only if failures are independent. The evidence shows that the house edge moves as the defender adapts, and that larger salvos can draw a stronger, concentrated defense. Volume gives predictability only after planners remove or measure the common factor.
- **Planners' tolerance is one-sided.** Planners guard against shortfall with 2–3 times more weapons than the expected need. With an overkill factor k, the plan still works if success is at least 1/k of plan, which is a shortfall tolerance of 50–67% for k = 2–3. Over-performance does not break a plan. The model's two-sided test (±tolerance) penalizes over-performance, which planners do not do.

## 5. How do the findings map to the model inputs?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports the acceptance range (0.7–0.95) and the ceiling on success per target (0.7), with care because both come from per-target kinetic standards. It narrows the tolerance: planners accept large shortfall margins bought with redundancy, they do not penalize over-performance, and they need tight timing (minutes) for scheduled enabling effects. It contradicts the one-week lower bound of campaign_time for the US "first mover" use, which happened in the opening hours. A yearly review is slower than wartime adaptation (weeks to months). Three yearly passes (3 years) are a little longer than the wartime cases (1–2 years) and much shorter than the peacetime cases (10–30 years).

### Cited Findings
- **acceptance (default 0.8; range 0.7–0.95).** Historical per-target assurance levels: 0.70, 0.75, 0.85, 0.90, 0.95 and 0.97 (SIOP-62/63 and SAC; [CIA 1961](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/1961-06-30a.pdf), [National Security Archive](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb236/index.htm), [Rathjens 1986](https://openvault.wgbh.org/catalog/V_9A93249305BB4FBC9CE4F8F6F52CCB56); search summaries; older). 0.90 in the 1941 bomber plan ([Correll 2008](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/2008/October%202008/1008daylight.pdf); full text; older). An illustrative Pd of 0.70 ([AFPAM 14-210, 1998](https://irp.fas.org/doddir/usaf/afpam14-210/part06.htm); older). → **Supports** the range; the default 0.8 sits in the middle. For effects on which other parts of an operation depend (the SEAD case), the evidence points to the upper half (0.9–0.95). Applies: transfer; these are per-target probabilities, not chances of an aggregate share.

- **tolerance (default 0.25; range 0.1–0.5).** No doctrine states a tolerance on the share of targets hit. Planners plan to expected fractions (Pd) and add redundancy (2–3 weapons per target; [National Security Archive, 2004](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB130/press.htm)). Realized efficiency differed from plan by large factors ([GAO 1997](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/report/gao/nsiad97134/summary.htm)). For a mass campaign, ±25% held in 50–69% of months and ±50% in 86–100% (my calculation from [ISIS, 2 June 2026](https://isis-online.org/uploads/isis-reports/documents/May-2026-Updated-Analysis-of-Russian-Shahed-type-UAVs-Deployment-Against-Ukraine_Final.pdf)). FPV success varies from 20% to 40% across fronts and crews, which is ±33% around 30% ([RUSI 2025](https://static.rusi.org/tactical-developments-third-year-russo-ukrainian-war-february-2205.pdf); [Kyiv Post 2024](https://www.kyivpost.com/post/44059)). Timing tolerance for enabling fires: about 1–2 minutes ([JP 3-09.3, 2014](https://irp.fas.org/doddir/dod/jp3_09_3.pdf); older). → **Narrows.** Keep 0.25 as the default and 0.5 as the upper end. The lower end of 0.1 is not plausible as a requirement for mass effects, because no weapon in this evidence holds ±10% month to month. Consider a one-sided test (shortfall only). Consider a separate timing tolerance for scheduled effects.

- **passes_needed (default 3; range 1–6) and review_interval (default 1 year).** Wartime: drone units across the front within about 1–2 years ([RUSI 2025](https://static.rusi.org/tactical-developments-third-year-russo-ukrainian-war-february-2205.pdf); Unmanned Systems Forces decree of 6 February 2024, search summaries); fixes in about 3 months and loss of effect within about a year ([The Washington Post, 2024](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2024/05/24/russian-jamming-leaves-some-high-tech-us-weapons-ineffective-in-ukraine/)); 21 months to fix the Mark 14 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_14_torpedo)). Peacetime: about 19–31 years for guided munitions ([Correll 2010](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0310bombs/)); about 4 years from a prototype Cyber JMEM to the first accredited increment (DOT&E, search summaries). → **Supports** 3 passes (3 years) for a competition at wartime intensity, where the cases took 1–2 years; the range up to 6 is **short** for peacetime institutional doctrine. The 1-year review is **slower** than wartime cycles: a quarterly review with 4–12 passes would match the evidence better, or the yearly review should use the worst quarter of the year. The evidence also **contradicts** a rule that only adds doctrine: doctrine can form from belief before evidence (US precision bombing in the 1930s) and can be dropped within months after failures (the British shift after the Butt Report; Ukraine stopped using Excalibur).

- **campaign_time (default 1 month; range 1 week–3 months).** The main 2003 air campaign lasted about 30 days with 29,199 munitions (USCENTAF, search summary). In Iran (2026), cyber effects came "before the attack began", and more than 1,000 targets were struck in the first 24 hours ([Al-Monitor (AFP), 2 March 2026](https://www.al-monitor.com/originals/2026/03/factbox-top-us-general-outlines-initial-timeline-us-military-operation-iran)). Russia's mass raids are single days or nights of 600–892 drones ([ISIS, 2 June 2026](https://isis-online.org/uploads/isis-reports/documents/May-2026-Updated-Analysis-of-Russian-Shahed-type-UAVs-Deployment-Against-Ukraine_Final.pdf)). → **Supports** 1 month for a campaign of attacks. **Contradicts** the lower bound for the documented US use: the opening phase lasts hours to one day, so the range should reach down to about 1 day.

- **success_best (default 0.7: the ceiling on success per target).** Mature precision weapons against weak countermeasures: 0.49 direct hits and 0.87 direct or within 25 feet (LGBs, 1972–73); 90% within 10 meters (B-2 JDAMs, 1999) ([Correll 2010](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0310bombs/)); about 0.9 (GBU-39 in Ukraine) and above 0.6 (JDAM after the fix) ([The Washington Post, 2024](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2024/05/24/russian-jamming-leaves-some-high-tech-us-weapons-ineffective-in-ukraine/)). Mass weapons under countermeasures: 0.2–0.4 (FPV), 0.03–0.19 (Shahed months), under 0.1 (Excalibur under jamming). WWII bombs within 1,000 feet: 0.16 (1943) rising to 0.60 (1945) ([Correll 2008](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/2008/October%202008/1008daylight.pdf)). → **Supports** 0.7 as a ceiling for a mature capability. A range of about 0.5–0.9 matches mature precision weapons. The realized value falls far below the ceiling when the defender adapts, so the ceiling alone does not set reliability. Applies: analogy.

- **Stages of the kill chain.** Planners multiply stage probabilities: the 1961 standard counted "all factors of attrition and reliability except pre-launch destruction" ([CIA 1961](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/1961-06-30a.pdf); search summary), and Pd is computed "assuming the probability of arrival" ([AFPAM 14-210](https://irp.fas.org/doddir/usaf/afpam14-210/part06.htm)). FPV drones: 0.2–0.4 reach the target, and fewer than half of those destroy an armored target ([RUSI 2025](https://static.rusi.org/tactical-developments-third-year-russo-ukrainian-war-february-2205.pdf)). The Sandworm effects chain: access (June 2022 or earlier), SCADA access for up to 3 months, capability built in about 2 months, capability held for about 3 weeks, the effect (10 October), then a wiper two days later ([Mandiant, 2023](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/sandworm-disrupts-power-ukraine-operational-technology)). JP 3-12 says "access is not guaranteed" and requires an intelligence gain/loss review before execution ([JP 3-12, 2018](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/media/16681/ocr)). → **Supports** separate stages. The effects chain has stages that the surveillance chain lacks: building the capability, a wait for the set time, the effect, and assessment. The two chains share the access stage, and the intelligence gain/loss review is the decision between them.

### Inferences
- **Suggested changes to the doctrine test (proposals only).**
  1. Make the tolerance one-sided (shortfall only), because planners do not penalize over-performance and they buy shortfall margin with redundancy.
  2. Add a timing condition for scheduled effects: the chance that the effect starts inside a window of minutes to hours around the set time. Doctrine for "a network down at a set hour" should need both the effect and the timing.
  3. Split doctrine into opening effects (hours to one day; US statements suggest that doctrine already exists in 2026) and campaign effects (one month; no public evidence of doctrine).
  4. Allow doctrine to lapse after failed reviews, as the Butt Report and the Excalibur cases show.
  5. Use the Shahed data to set the common factor: a month-to-month log standard deviation of about 0.24–0.41 for a mass campaign against a defender that adapts.
- **How directly this applies.** The acceptance and timing numbers transfer from kinetic and nuclear planning. The drone and Shahed numbers transfer from mass weapons under countermeasures. Only the 2026 statements, JP 3-12, the Cyber JMEM status and the Sandworm and Viasat cases are direct evidence about capable cyber forces, and none of them gives a reliability number.

### Gaps
- No public source gives the reliability, the timing spread or the success rate of US cyber effects.
- The SIOP percentages, the GAO figures, the Iraq 2003 counts, the Unmanned Systems Forces dates and the Venezuela statements come from search summaries, not from my own reading of the primary documents.
- The search budget ran out before I could check early tanks, First World War submarines, air-defense salvo doctrine, field artillery effect criteria, Grissom (2006) and Posen (1984).
