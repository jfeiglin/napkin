# Zero-day economics as public statistics: lifetimes, rediscovery, reuse, needs per operation, one-day substitution and burns (IT vs industrial)

Scope: coarse, public, strategic statistics only (counts, shares, rates, times). No exploit or technique detail. Research date: 2026-11-08. Each finding gives the value, the population and setting, the date of the figure, a quality grade and the SC45 input it could set.
Quality grades: **A** = primary source, systematic dataset. **B** = primary source, but vendor telemetry with selection bias, a small sample or a single case. **C** = free secondary summary of a primary source that I could not fetch. **D** = conflicting or unverified secondary sources (use only as a pointer).
SC45 inputs today (for mapping): one-day share of needs 80% (access, effect) and 90% (movement, collection); defender patch rate 1.5/yr with 25% never, industrial 0.3/yr, inside-network 0.75/yr; rediscovery of a held flaw 14.5%/yr; 80% of attacks exposed and exposure burns capabilities; 10% of an attack's zero-days shared with surveillance, 5% from an earlier attack; surveillance meets 95% of its zero-day needs from capabilities it already uses; 2% of accesses found when a shared capability burns; 10% chance that a further target runs a new kind of system.

## Q1. How long do held zero-days last, how fast do others find and disclose them, and how many are caught in the wild each year?

### Takeaway
RAND's private stockpile data (207 exploits, 2002–2016) give an independent-rediscovery ("collision") rate of 5.7% per year, but an average time to public disclosure of 6.9 years, which is a hazard of 1/6.9 = 14.5% per year. SC45's 14.5% matches the second number, not RAND's own rediscovery figure. Bug-bounty data for heavily researched consumer products give higher rediscovery (10.8–21.9%). Zero-days caught in use are a different, much faster process: about 60–100 are caught each year worldwide (90 in 2025), and a zero-day in use lasted about 312 days on average before disclosure in 2008–2011 telemetry.

### Cited Findings
- **Dataset:** 207 exploits and their vulnerabilities over 14 years (2002–2016), from "BUSBY", a private research group used as a proxy for a nation-state; 4% of the vulnerabilities were bought from an outside third party. Figure date: 2017. Quality A− (one private dataset, with selection bias). Model use: the base population for the held-zero-day loss rates. — [Ablon & Bogart, Black Hat USA slides (Jul 2017)](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf); full report [RAND RR1751 (Mar 2017)](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR1751.html) (the RAND page returned HTTP 403, so I verified the figures from the authors' slides)
- **Average life expectancy 6.9 years.** RAND defines longevity as how long a zero-day stays "undiscovered and undisclosed to the public". Short life is under 1.51 years (75% survival probability), and long life is over 9.53 years (25% survival probability). Figure date: 2017 (2002–2016 data). Quality A−. Model use: an all-route loss rate for a held flaw, ≈ 1/6.9 = 0.145/yr. — [Ablon & Bogart slides (Jul 2017)](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf)
- **Collision (independent rediscovery and public disclosure) rate: 5.7% per 365-day interval, 0.87% per 90-day interval and 40% over the whole 14 years.** The slides headline 5.7% per year as the "percentage for likelihood of independent rediscovery". The method takes the median, over all intervals, of the share of a refreshed stockpile that others found and disclosed. "Collision rates change significantly depending on the interval time." Figure date: 2017. Quality A−. Model use: the direct estimate for "others find and disclose a held flaw". — [Ablon & Bogart slides (Jul 2017)](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf); same figures in [CyberScoop (Mar 2017)](https://cyberscoop.com/study-hoarded-zero-days-last-seven-years-and-are-rarely-discovered/)
- **Caveat:** RAND could measure only collisions that became public. Rediscoveries that other parties kept private do not appear in the data. Figure date: Mar 2017. Quality B. — [CyberScoop (Mar 2017)](https://cyberscoop.com/study-hoarded-zero-days-last-seven-years-and-are-rarely-discovered/)
- **Exploit development time:** "Over 70% of exploits are developed in a month (31 days) or less." Figure date: 2017 (2002–2016 data). Quality A−. Model use: lead time to turn a found flaw into a capability. — [Ablon & Bogart slides (Jul 2017)](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf)
- **Rediscovery in public bug data (Herr, Schneier & Morris, "Taking Stock"):** the revised 2017 version, with more than 2,600 vulnerabilities, gives an aggregate rediscovery rate of 12.7%, from 10.8% (Chrome, 2009–2017) to 21.9% (Android, 2016–2017). In Android and Chrome, more than 60% of rediscovery takes place "in the first month after the original vulnerability's disclosure". The original July 2017 version (more than 4,300 vulnerabilities) gave Android 13.9% within 60 days, 20% within 90 days and more than 21% within 120 days. Figure date: 2017. Quality C (I checked the abstract through search summaries, not the full text). The data come from bug-bounty products with heavy competition. Model use: an upper range of rediscovery for mainstream browser and mobile targets. — [Belfer Center](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/taking-stock-estimating-vulnerability-rediscovery); [Schneier.com academic page](https://www.schneier.com/academic/archives/2017/07/taking_stock_estimat.html); [Schneier blog (Jul 2017)](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2017/07/measuring_vulne.html)
- **Zero-days in use (Bilge & Dumitras, "Before We Knew It", CCS 2012):** 18 vulnerabilities exploited before disclosure (2008–2011), found in telemetry from about 11 million hosts. A zero-day attack lasted 312 days on average before public disclosure (range 19 days to 30 months). After disclosure, attack volume rose by up to five orders of magnitude, mostly within 30 days. Figure date: 2012. Quality B (old data, small sample, one vendor's telemetry). Model use: the exposure hazard of a capability while it is in use (≈ 1.2/yr), and the jump in one-day use after a burn. — [Threatpost (Oct 2012)](https://threatpost.com/zero-day-attacks-thrive-months-disclosure-101612/77118); [Bilge & Dumitras, USENIX ;login: summary](https://www.usenix.net/system/files/login/articles/02_bilge_6-13_online.pdf)
- **Zero-days exploited in the wild per year (GTIG, the latest revisions):** 2025: 90. 2024: 78 (first reported as 75). 2023: 100 (first reported as 97, then 98). 2022: 63 (also given as 62). 2021: 106 (the record in the 2023 review). GTIG says 2021–2025 "fluctuated within a 60–100 range" and that counts "may adjust" as GTIG finds earlier incidents. Figure dates: data years 2021–2025, published Mar 2024 to Mar 2026. Quality A− (only detected and disclosed cases; counts rise with later revisions). Model use: the global yearly flow of zero-days that are caught and burned. — [GTIG 2025 review (Mar 5, 2026)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/); [GTIG 2024 review (2025)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2024-zero-day-trends); [Google 2023 review (Mar 27, 2024)](https://blog.google/technology/safety-security/a-review-of-zero-day-in-the-wild-exploits-in-2023/)
- **Narrower tracker:** Project Zero alone counted 41 in-the-wild 0-days in 2022. More than 40% of them (17 of 41) were variants of earlier reported vulnerabilities. In mid-2022, at least 9 of 18 were variants of previously patched bugs. Figure date: data 2022, published Jul 2023. Quality A− for counts. Model use: burned flaw families regenerate as new zero-days. A count series needs one consistent tracker (the GTIG and Project Zero counts differ). — [Google Security Blog (Jul 27, 2023)](https://security.googleblog.com/2023/07/the-ups-and-downs-of-0-days-year-in.html?m=1); [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/google-half-2022s-zero-days-are-variants-previous-vulnerabilities/); [Project Zero mid-2022 (Jun 2022)](https://projectzero.google/2022/06/2022-0-day-in-wild-exploitationso-far.html)
- **Patch-gap example (unverified detail):** a search summary of Google's July 2023 post says that a GPU driver flaw patched upstream in January 2022 was still used as a 0-day about 11 months later against devices that had not received the fix. Quality D. Model use: an upstream patch is not a fix on the target. — [Google Security Blog (Jul 2023)](https://security.googleblog.com/2023/07/the-ups-and-downs-of-0-days-year-in.html?m=1)
- **Forecast, not a measurement:** GTIG expects adversaries to "utilize AI to automate and scale attacks by accelerating reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, and exploit development". It also expects AI agents to help vendors find and fix flaws before exploitation. Figure date: Mar 2026. Quality B (an opinion). Model use: a direction for 2026–2051 rediscovery and development rates. — [GTIG 2025 review (Mar 2026)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/)

### Inferences
- I converted RAND's statistics to annual hazards. Collision: 5.7%/365 days → λ = −ln(0.943) = 0.059/yr. 0.87%/90 days → 1 − 0.9913^(365/90) = 3.5%/yr. 40% over 14 years → −ln(0.6)/14 = 0.036/yr (3.6%/yr). Longevity: 1/6.9 = 0.145/yr. 75% surviving 1.51 years → −ln(0.75)/1.51 = 0.19/yr in the first 1.5 years. 25% surviving 9.53 years → −ln(0.25)/9.53 = 0.145/yr. The longevity curve is therefore close to exponential at about 0.145/yr, with a slightly higher early hazard.
- **SC45's 14.5%/yr equals RAND's 1/6.9 all-route disclosure hazard.** RAND's own "likelihood of independent rediscovery" is 3.5–5.7%/yr, depending on the interval. If the input means "others find and disclose", the RAND basis supports about 4–6%/yr for a state-class stockpile. 14.5%/yr fits a broader "stops being a private zero-day" rate. For heavily researched consumer platforms, the Herr et al. data support 11–22%/yr. One option is to split the input by target class, or to state that 14.5% is an all-route loss rate.
- A held, unused flaw (about 0.06–0.15/yr) and a flaw in use (Bilge & Dumitras: about 1/0.85 yr ≈ 1.2/yr) have loss hazards that differ by about one order of magnitude. Use is therefore the main driver of burns, which is consistent with SC45's exposure-driven burn.
- More than 40% of 2022's in-the-wild 0-days were variants of earlier bugs. A burned capability can thus often be rebuilt from the same flaw family, which lowers the replacement cost after a burn.
- GTIG revises counts upward later (2023 went 97 → 98 → 100; 2024 went 75 → 78), so a recent year's count is a lower bound.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the RAND full report (HTTP 403). Per-platform life expectancy, the median exploit development time and the "immortal" share were not in the slide text I extracted.
- I found no post-2017 study that repeats RAND's stockpile collision measurement on newer data (for example, with AI-era discovery rates).
- I did not fetch the full text of Herr et al.: the definition of their "rediscovery rate" window (annual or per dataset) is unconfirmed.

## Q2. How often are zero-days reused across operations and actors, how often do espionage and disruptive attacks share them, and what share do state espionage actors use?

### Takeaway
Espionage actors (states plus commercial surveillance vendors, CSVs) used most attributed in-the-wild zero-days: about 83% of attributed cases in 2023, 53% in 2024, and in 2025 the CSVs alone exceeded traditional state espionage for the first time. Sharing is well documented as cases but not as a rate. Examples are PRC groups sharing exploits before a patch (Exchange 2021: five clusters before the patch, more than 10 in total), an APT reusing CSV exploits as n-days, a state-linked exploit pair reused by a criminal group, and an espionage tool whose zero-days later appeared in a sabotage weapon (Fanny → Stuxnet). The most active state program (PRC) loses about 5–12 attributed zero-days per year.

### Cited Findings
- **2023 attribution:** 97 zero-days in 2023. Government-backed actors accounted for 48 attributed zero-days, and half of them (24 of 48) were CSVs and their government customers. PRC espionage: 12 (up from 7 in 2022). Financially motivated actors: 10 (FIN11 3; at least four ransomware groups 4). CSVs were behind 75% (13 of 17) of zero-days that targeted Google products and Android, and behind more than 60% of the 37 browser and mobile zero-days. Figure date: data 2023, published Mar 27, 2024. Quality A− (attribution covers only part of the total). Model use: the espionage share of zero-day demand; CSVs as a shared supply pool. — [Google 2023 review (Mar 27, 2024)](https://blog.google/technology/safety-security/a-review-of-zero-day-in-the-wild-exploits-in-2023/); [Infosecurity Magazine](https://infosecurity-magazine.com/news/zeroday-surged-50-annually-google); [TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/news/366575693/Spyware-vendors-behind-75-of-zero-days-targeting-Google)
- **2024 attribution:** GTIG attributed 34 of 75 zero-days ("just under half"; about 30% were attributed in 2022). Espionage (state plus CSV) was "nearly 53% (18 vulnerabilities)": 10 by likely nation-state groups and 8 by CSVs. PRC: 5, all against security and networking products. North Korea: 5 (the first tie with the PRC since tracking began in 2012). Financially motivated: about 15% (5). Mixed motivation (CIGAR, "likely on behalf of the Russian government"): 2. The categories that GTIG names sum to 25 of 34, and its statement that the PRC had "nearly 30% of traditional espionage zero-days" does not fit 5 of 10. Figure date: data 2024, published 2025. Quality A− (with internal inconsistencies). — [GTIG 2024 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2024-zero-day-trends)
- **2025 attribution:** "For the first time … we attributed more exploitation to CSVs than to traditional state-sponsored cyber espionage groups" (GTIG gives no count). PRC espionage: at least 10 (double 2024, below the 12 of 2023). North Korea: 0. Financially motivated: 9 (2 led to ransomware). "Just over half" of attributed state espionage zero-day exploitation focused on edge and security devices. Figure date: data 2025, published Mar 5, 2026. Quality A−. — [GTIG 2025 review (Mar 2026)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/)
- **Shared exploit supply in the PRC:** PRC-nexus groups are "increasingly adept at developing, sharing, and distributing exploits among themselves", and GTIG observed mass exploitation. The time from n-day disclosure to "widespread exploitation by multiple groups" is "shrinking". In 2025, one archive-tool zero-day was used by a financially motivated cluster and by a cluster that overlaps RomCom. Figure date: Mar 2026. Quality A− (qualitative; no count). Model use: the share of an actor's zero-days that other actors also hold. — [GTIG 2025 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/)
- **Exchange 2021 (ProxyLogon) as a shared-zero-day case:** ESET found "more than 10 different threat actors". Five clusters were active before the patch of Mar 2, 2021 (the first on Feb 28). Further clusters started within 1–3 days after the patch. More than 5,000 servers in over 115 countries had webshells (ESET called this incomplete). ESET concluded that "multiple threat actors had access to the vulnerability details before the patch". Exploitation reportedly began on Jan 3, 2021, two days before the flaw was reported to the vendor (Jan 5). Figure date: Mar 10, 2021. Quality B (one vendor's telemetry). Model use: a zero-day shared among several state groups before burn, then mass one-day use after burn. — [ESET WeLiveSecurity (Mar 10, 2021)](https://www.welivesecurity.com/2021/03/10/exchange-servers-under-siege-10-apt-groups/)
- **APT reuse of CSV exploits:** suspected APT29 (moderate confidence) ran three watering-hole iterations on Mongolian government sites (Nov 2023, Feb 2024, Jul 2024). The exploits were "identical or strikingly similar" to Intellexa and NSO exploits. The same iOS exploit served two iterations three months apart. The Chrome pair was used about two months after it was reported as an in-the-wild 0-day (May → late Jul 2024). "APT actors are using n-day exploits that were originally used as 0-days by CSVs." The acquisition route is unknown. Figure date: Aug 29, 2024. Quality B (a case). Model use: surveillance capability reuse across operations; a burned capability becomes another actor's one-day. — [Google TAG (Aug 29, 2024)](https://blog.google/threat-analysis-group/state-backed-attackers-and-commercial-surveillance-vendors-repeatedly-use-the-same-exploits/)
- **State-linked exploits reused by a criminal group while still a zero-day:** the 2024 CIGAR browser chain (two vulnerabilities) was also used by "another, likely financially motivated, group" with a different payload, "while CVE-2024-49039 was still a zero-day". Figure date: 2025. Quality B. — [GTIG 2024 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2024-zero-day-trends)
- **Espionage → sabotage sharing (Fanny → Stuxnet):** in 2008, the Fanny worm (used to map air-gapped networks) used two zero-days that "were introduced into Stuxnet in June 2009 and March 2010", including the shortcut (LNK) flaw. Kaspersky concluded that the developers were the same group or worked closely together, and it did not name a state. Figure date: Feb 2015. Quality B (vendor case analysis). Model use: a documented case where sabotage zero-days came from an espionage pool. — [Kaspersky press release (Feb 2015)](https://www.kaspersky.com/about/press-releases/equation-group-the-crown-creator-of-cyber-espionage); [Threatpost](https://threatpost.com/massive-decades-long-cyberespionage-framework-uncovered/111080)
- **Stuxnet's four zero-days:** Symantec's dossier (v1.4, Feb 2011) lists 4 zero-day exploits: two for self-replication (one is the shortcut flaw, later MS10-046) and two for privilege escalation. Figure date: 2010–2011. Quality B (I saw the dossier only through copies and testimony). — [Symantec dossier page](https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/stuxnet-dossier-espionage); [Turner, Senate testimony (Nov 17, 2010)](https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/TestimonyTurner20101117REVISED2.pdf); [Defense One (Feb 2011)](https://www.defenseone.com/defense-systems/2011/02/stuxnet-had-a-full-bag-of-tricks-report-says/193013/)
- **Leaked espionage capability → disruptive use (EternalBlue):** the vendor patch was published on Mar 14, 2017. The Shadow Brokers leaked the tool set on Apr 14, 2017. WannaCry used the exploit in May 2017, and NotPetya used the same exploit for internal spread in June 2017. Figure date: 2017. Quality C (vendor explainers; the facts are widely documented). Model use: after a leak, a burned espionage capability served as a one-day for other states' disruptive attacks. — [AVG explainer](https://avg.com/en/signal/eternal-blue); [TitanHQ on NotPetya](https://www.titanhq.com/blog/notpetya-a-ransomware-attack-with-worm-like-behaviour-but-without-the-ranso/)
- **Bought capabilities:** 4% of the vulnerabilities in the RAND/BUSBY dataset were bought from an outside third party. Figure date: 2017. Quality A− (one group). — [Ablon & Bogart slides (Jul 2017)](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf)

### Inferences
- The espionage share of attributed zero-days fell from about 48/(48+10) ≈ 83% (2023, counting only the two named groups) to 53% (2024), and it stayed dominant in 2025, with CSVs as the largest single source. Attribution covers only about half of the total (34 of 75 in 2024), and the detection is biased towards the platforms of the reporting vendors (Google, mobile, browsers).
- The detected burn flow of the most active state (PRC espionage) was 7, 12, 5 and ≥10 zero-days per year (2022–2025), a mean of about 8.5 per year. This is a lower bound on what a major state loses each year, because about half of all zero-days have no attribution. It can check an SC45 run: (exposed operations per year) × (zero-days per operation) should give the same order of magnitude for a major state.
- **Espionage–attack sharing (SC45's 10%):** the only public state sabotage case with a documented pool (Stuxnet) shows that at least 1 of its 4 zero-days (≥25%) had been used about two years earlier by an espionage tool. This is one case, not a rate. Disruptive attacks after 2016 that are documented in public sources mostly used one-days or native functions (see Q3), so the share for an average attack could be well below 10%. The 10% stays an unreviewed assumption, with a case-based upper bound near 25–50%.
- **Reuse of an earlier attack's zero-days (SC45's 5%):** I found no public example of a disruptive attack reusing an unburned zero-day from an earlier disruptive attack. All documented reuse is either espionage-to-espionage (APT29, PRC sharing) or the use of a burned or leaked capability as a one-day (EternalBlue). The 5% stays an assumption.
- **Surveillance meeting 95% of zero-day needs from its own pool:** this is consistent with long held lifetimes (6.9 years), low collision (≈6%/yr), the reuse of one iOS exploit across two campaigns, and the CSV model, where one vendor exploit serves many government customers. No public rate exists.

### Gaps
- I found no statistic for the share of zero-days used by more than one actor, or in more than one campaign. GTIG gives cases but no count.
- I found no 2025 count of CSV-attributed zero-days, and no attributed or unattributed totals for 2025.
- The 2024 GTIG attribution categories do not sum to the total, and the PRC share statement is inconsistent. A primary table would resolve this.

## Q3. How many zero-days does an operation need, and what share of intrusions use zero-days, known flaws or credentials?

### Takeaway
Exploits are the top initial vector in Mandiant cases (32% in 2025), and the 2026 DBIR reportedly also puts exploitation first for the first time (about 31%; the secondary sources conflict). Most exploited flaws are n-days by count (about 71% of the 884 newly exploited CVEs in 2025 were first exploited after the CVE publication day). Among high-profile tracked flaws, most are zero-days (70% in 2023). Enterprise and edge targets usually need a single flaw. Mobile and browser targets need chains of 2–4 flaws (about 90% of multi-zero-day chains target mobile). Industrial attacks in public records used 0–4 zero-days, and the zero-days often served spreading and privilege rather than the physical effect.

### Cited Findings
- **Chains by target class (2024):** multi-zero-day chains were "almost exclusively (~90%) used to target mobile devices". For enterprise products, "exploit chains are not generally required … individual vulnerabilities can single-handedly achieve remote code execution or privilege escalation". The browser chains described had 2 flaws, and one physical-access forensic chain had 4. Figure date: data 2024, published 2025. Quality A−. Model use: capabilities needed per access step, by system class (enterprise or edge ≈ 1; mobile or browser ≈ 2–4). — [GTIG 2024 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2024-zero-day-trends)
- **Chains (2025):** "multiple exploit chains discovered in 2025 included three or more vulnerabilities". Actors also succeeded "with fewer or singular bugs by targeting lower levels of access". One edge-appliance chain combined two n-days with one zero-day. Figure date: Mar 2026. Quality A−. Model use: one-days and zero-days serve different steps of the same operation. — [GTIG 2025 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/)
- **Product mix (2025):** enterprise 43 (48%, an all-time high) and end-user 47 (52%). Security and networking: 21 (about half of enterprise). Edge devices: 14 ("likely underrepresents"). Operating systems: 39 (44%). Mobile: 15. Browsers: under 10%. About 35% were memory-safety flaws. "20 vendors who were exploited by just one zero-day each." 2024: enterprise 33 (44%) [revised to 36, 46%], security and networking 20 (more than 60% of enterprise), 18 enterprise vendors out of 20 in total. 2023: enterprise 36 (37%). Figure dates: 2023–2025. Quality A−. — [GTIG 2025 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/); [GTIG 2024 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2024-zero-day-trends)
- **Initial infection vectors (Mandiant, 2025 cases, more than 500,000 investigation hours):** exploits 32% (the top vector for the sixth year), voice phishing 11%, prior compromise 10% (30% in ransomware cases, up from 15%), email phishing 6% and stolen credentials 9%. Figure date: data 2025, published Apr 2026. Quality A− for the dataset (incident-response cases; shares of cases with a known vector), but I got the stolen-credential share from a secondary source (C). Model use: the share of access steps that need a flaw at all (about one third), with the rest served by people or credentials. — [M-Trends 2026 (Google Cloud, Apr 2026)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/m-trends-2026); [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/m-trends-2026-initial-access-handoff-shrinks-from-hours-to-22-seconds/)
- **Dwell and detection (Mandiant, 2025):** the global median dwell time was 14 days (11 in 2024). The median for cyber-espionage cases was 122 days. Organizations detected the intrusion themselves in 52% of cases (43% in 2024). The median hand-off from the initial-access group to a second group fell from more than 8 hours (2022) to 22 seconds (2025). Figure date: Apr 2026. Quality A−. Model use: exposure hazards; access sharing between groups. — [M-Trends 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/m-trends-2026)
- **Verizon DBIR 2025:** exploitation of vulnerabilities was the initial access vector in about 20% of breaches. Edge devices and VPNs rose from 3% to 22% of the exploitation vector. Figure date: Apr 2025. Quality C (vendor summaries of the DBIR). — [Tenable snapshot (Apr 25, 2025)](https://www.tenable.com/blog/cybersecurity-snapshot-verizon-dbir-zeroday-vulnerability-exploits-surge-vpn-edge-device-04-25-2025); [Qualys (Apr 22, 2025)](https://blog.qualys.com/qualys-insights/2025/04/22/the-verizon-2025-data-breach-investigations-report-dbir-six-trends-you-cant-ignore)
- **Verizon DBIR 2026:** exploitation reportedly became the leading initial access vector for the first time, most often reported as 31% (up from 20%). Other secondary sources give 34% or 36%. Figure date: May 2026. Quality D (secondary sources disagree; the primary PDF was not fetched, and TechRepublic returned 403). — [watchTowr](https://watchtowr.com/resources/verizon-dbir-2026-vulnerability-exploitation/); [PT Security dbugs (May 26, 2026)](https://dbugs.ptsecurity.com/news/verizon-dbir-2026-exploitation-of-vulnerabilities-becomes-the-leading-initial-access-vector-20260526)
- **Zero-day vs n-day among tracked exploited flaws (Mandiant):** 138 vulnerabilities disclosed in 2023 and exploited, of which 97 (70%) were zero-days and 41 (30%) n-days. The ratio was 62:38 in 2021–2022 and 61:39 in 2020–2021. There were 53 exploited vendors, and 31 of them (58%) had only one exploited vulnerability. Figure date: data 2023, published Oct 15, 2024. Quality A− (a curated set of high-confidence, high-profile flaws). Model use: the zero-day share of flaws used for exploitation. — [Mandiant TTE 2023 (Oct 15, 2024)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/time-to-exploit-trends-2023)
- **Zero-day vs n-day across all newly exploited flaws (VulnCheck):** 884 CVEs first showed exploitation evidence in 2025, and 28.96% of them had evidence on or before the day of CVE publication (23.6% in 2024; 32.1% of 432 in the first half of 2025). "Not all KEVs being exploited on the same day of CVE issuance are Zero Days." These covered 518 vendors and 672 products. CISA KEV added 245 in the same year (99 vendors, 146 products). Network edge devices were the most targeted category. Figure date: Jan 21, 2026. Quality B (a broad but heterogeneous evidence base). — [VulnCheck State of Exploitation 2026 (Jan 21, 2026)](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/state-of-exploitation-2026); [VulnCheck 1H 2025](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/state-of-exploitation-1h-2025)
- **Industrial case: TRITON/TRISIS (2017):** the attack on a safety controller used one zero-day, a privilege-escalation flaw in a few older firmware versions, which Schneider Electric confirmed on Jan 18, 2018. Figure date: Jan 2018. Quality B. Model use: an OT effect step served by one zero-day. — [Dark Reading (Jan 2018)](https://www.darkreading.com/vulnerabilities-threats/schneider-electric-triton-trisis-attack-used-0-day-flaw-in-its-safety-controller-system-and-a-rat); [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/schneider-electric-trisis-zero-day-rat/); [DTIC report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/trecms/pdf/AD1183008.pdf)
- **Industrial case: Industroyer (Dec 2016 Ukraine grid attack, as ESET assessed it, "highly probable"):** its denial-of-service component used CVE-2015-5374, a flaw in a protection relay with a 2015 identifier, so it was at least about one year old when the attack used it. The rest of the tool set used the grid protocols themselves. Figure date: Jun 12, 2017. Quality B. Model use: an OT effect step served by a one-day plus native functions. — [ESET WeLiveSecurity (Jun 12, 2017)](https://www.welivesecurity.com/2017/06/12/industroyer-biggest-threat-industrial-control-systems-since-stuxnet/); [MITRE ATT&CK S0604](https://attack.MITRE.org/software/S0604)
- **Industrial case: Stuxnet:** 4 zero-days, two for spreading and two for privilege escalation (see Q2). Figure date: 2010–2011. Quality B. — [Turner, Senate testimony (Nov 2010)](https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/TestimonyTurner20101117REVISED2.pdf)
- **OT intrusion routes (Dragos field data):** 73% of all-time Dragos incident-response cases involved compromised VPN or jump-host credentials. 56% of penetration tests abused living-off-the-land tools without alerts. 81% of assessments found poor IT/OT segmentation. Figure date: Feb 17, 2026 (mixed years). Quality B (one vendor's case mix). Model use: OT movement and collection depend mostly on credentials and native tools, not flaws. — [Dragos 2026 Year in Review blog (Feb 17, 2026)](https://www.dragos.com/blog/dragos-2026-ot-cybersecurity-year-in-review)

### Inferences
- Zero-days are about 90/884 ≈ 10% of the flaws first seen exploited in 2025, and about 29% of new KEVs had evidence on or before the day of CVE publication. By count, one-days do most of the exploitation work. Among high-profile flaws (Mandiant's set), the zero-day share is about 60–70%, so the actors that matter most for SC45 (state espionage on edge devices, CSVs on mobile) are more dependent on zero-days than the average.
- **Needs per operation by class (coarse):** enterprise or edge access ≈ 1 flaw. Mobile or browser access ≈ 2–4 flaws (chains). Movement in IT and OT is often met by credentials or native tools (Dragos: 73% credentials). Industrial effect: 0 zero-days (Industroyer: a one-day plus native protocol use), 1 (TRITON) or 4 (Stuxnet, where the zero-days served spreading and privilege, which are movement steps in SC45 terms, not the physical effect itself).
- SC45's 80% (access) and 90% (movement, collection) one-day shares get directional support for enterprise, edge and movement steps. The 80% for access may be high against hardened mobile or browser targets, where zero-days were 61–70% of the high-profile exploited flaws that Mandiant tracked in 2020–2023. No public statistic measures "the share of needs a one-day can serve" directly.
- Only about one third of initial access uses any exploit (Mandiant 32%). If SC45 counts only flaw-based steps, the other two thirds (phishing, credentials, prior compromise) are outside the capability pool. If SC45 counts all access steps, it should add a "no flaw needed" share.

### Gaps
- I found no public statistic of zero-days per operation (only per-chain cases), and no zero-day share of intrusions (as distinct from the share of exploited vulnerabilities).
- The DBIR 2026 primary figures were not verified (403 and conflicting secondary sources).
- A claim in one secondary source that 81% of CVEs first exploited in 2025 had been disclosed before 2025 could not be traced to a primary source.
- No dataset splits needs by kill-chain step (access, movement, effect, collection).

## Q4. How fast are one-days exploited after disclosure, and how fast do defenders patch, by system class (IT, edge, inside the network, industrial)?

### Takeaway
Attackers now exploit flaws faster than defenders patch them. The mean time-to-exploit fell from 63 days (2018–2019) to 5 days (2023), and to an estimated −7 days (2025). 56% of n-days are exploited within a month and 95% within six months. On the defender side, the median remediation time for CISA-KEV flaws was 174 days (2023 data), which equals about 1.45/yr and matches SC45's 1.5/yr. Flaws outside the KEV list take 621 days (≈0.41/yr). For critical edge KEVs, only 54% were fully remediated in the DBIR 2025 period. In industrial systems, 25% of 2025 advisories had no patch or mitigation, 71% of flaws wait for the next maintenance cycle, and about 12% of OT devices carry a KEV.

### Cited Findings
- **Time-to-exploit trend (Mandiant):** the mean time-to-exploit was 63 days (2018–2019), 44 days (2020 to early 2021), 32 days (2021–2022) and 5 days (2023; 47 days if 15 outliers are kept). Figure date: Oct 15, 2024. Quality A−. — [Mandiant TTE 2023 (Oct 15, 2024)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/time-to-exploit-trends-2023)
- **n-day exploitation delay (2023):** 12% (5) were exploited within 1 day, 29% (12) within 1 week, 56% within 1 month and 95% within 6 months (in the previous report, 25% came after 6 months). Median disclosure → exploitation was 43 days when a public exploit appeared first and 15 days when exploitation came first. Mandiant found "a distinct lack of association" between public exploit release and exploitation. Mandiant calls these "conservative" figures (true times are earlier). Figure date: Oct 15, 2024. Quality A−. Model use: how soon after disclosure a one-day becomes usable by other actors. — [Mandiant TTE 2023](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/time-to-exploit-trends-2023)
- **Mean time-to-exploit (2025):** an estimated −7 days, "meaning exploitation often occurs before a patch is released". Figure date: Apr 2026. Quality A− (method not given in the blog). — [M-Trends 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/m-trends-2026)
- **Edge devices (DBIR 2025):** for 17 critical edge-device KEVs (seven vendors), only 54% were fully remediated during the reporting period. The median time to remediate was 32 days (38 days for all KEVs). The median time to mass exploitation was 0 days. Figure date: Apr 2025. Quality C (vendor summaries of the DBIR; the Tenable data partnership). Model use: the patch rate and the "never or late" tail for perimeter systems. — [Tenable (2025 DBIR collaboration)](https://www.tenable.com/blog/verizon-2025-dbir-tenable-research-collaboration); [Tenable snapshot (Apr 25, 2025)](https://www.tenable.com/blog/cybersecurity-snapshot-verizon-dbir-zeroday-vulnerability-exploits-surge-vpn-edge-device-04-25-2025)
- **KEV remediation across organizations (Bitsight, 2023 data, 1.4 million organizations):** the median time to remediate was 174 days for KEV-listed flaws and 621 days for others (3.5× slower). By severity, critical KEVs took 137 days, high 238 days and medium 517 days. More than 60% of KEVs were remediated after the CISA deadlines. Federal agencies were 56% more likely than private organizations to meet a deadline. Figure date: May 2024. Quality B (one vendor's observation method; probably internet-facing assets, which I did not confirm). Model use: the base defender patch rate. — [Bitsight report PDF (2024)](https://www.bitsight.com/sites/default/files/2024-04/bitsight-a-global-view-of-cisa-kev-catalog.pdf); [Bitsight press release](https://www.bitsight.com/press-releases/bitsight-reveals-more-60-percent-known-exploited-vulnerabilities-remain-unmitigated); [The Record](https://therecord.media/kev-list-vulnerabilities-patched-significantly-faster); [Help Net Security (May 13, 2024)](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2024/05/13/kev-catalog-prevalent-vulnerabilities); [The Register (May 7, 2024)](https://www.theregister.com/2024/05/07/cisas_vulnerability_deadlines/)
- **KEV coverage gap:** VulnCheck listed 884 newly exploited CVEs in 2025, and CISA KEV added 245, so about 72% of the flaws newly seen exploited were not in CISA's catalogue that year. Figure date: Jan 21, 2026. Quality B. Model use: most one-days do not get the KEV-driven patch speed-up. — [VulnCheck State of Exploitation 2026](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/state-of-exploitation-2026)
- **OT exposure (Claroty Team82, about 1 million OT devices in 270 organizations):** about 12% of devices had at least one KEV (more than 111,000 KEVs in total). 40% of organizations had some of these assets insecurely connected to the internet. 7% of devices had KEVs linked to ransomware, and 31% of organizations had those assets insecurely connected. Manufacturing had the largest share. Figure date: Feb 2025. Quality B (customer base of one vendor). Model use: how common unpatched known flaws are in OT, which supports a slow industrial patch rate. — [Claroty press release (Feb 2025)](https://blog.claroty.com/press-releases/new-research-from-clarotys-team82-highlights-urgent-ransomware-and-insecure-connectivity-threats-to-mission-critical-ot-assets); [BetaNews (Feb 4, 2025)](https://betanews.com/2025/02/04/operational-tech-devices-exposed-to-known-vulnerabilities)
- **OT advisories (Dragos, 2025 data):** 25% of advisories had no patch or mitigation, 52% needed alternative mitigations from Dragos, and 4% were actively exploited. Triage: "Now" 3%, "Next" (compensating controls or the next maintenance cycle) 71% and "Never" (no remediation effort warranted) 27%. In several incident-response cases, Dragos waited "90+ days for public disclosure" of actively exploited flaws while attacks continued elsewhere. Figure date: Feb 17, 2026. Quality B. Model use: the industrial patch rate and the "never" share. — [Dragos 2026 Year in Review blog (Feb 17, 2026)](https://www.dragos.com/blog/dragos-2026-ot-cybersecurity-year-in-review)
- **OT advisories (Dragos, 2024 data):** 26% of advisories had no patch, and 18% had no patch and no mitigation. Figure date: Feb 2025. Quality C. — [TechTarget (Feb 2025)](https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/news/366619652/Dragos-Ransomware-attacks-against-industrial-orgs-up-87)

### Inferences
- I converted the medians to exponential patch rates with λ = ln2 / median. Bitsight KEV 174 days → 1.45/yr (matches SC45's 1.5/yr). Critical KEV 137 days → 1.85/yr. High 238 days → 1.06/yr. Medium 517 days → 0.49/yr. Non-KEV 621 days → 0.41/yr. DBIR 2025 edge KEV 32 days → 7.9/yr and all KEVs 38 days → 6.7/yr. The DBIR medians probably cover only flaws remediated within the period (about 54% for edge), so they overstate the speed for the whole population.
- SC45's 1.5/yr fits flaws that are listed as known-exploited (KEV), on internet-facing assets. Because about 72% of newly exploited flaws in 2025 were not in CISA KEV, a population-weighted one-day patch rate may be closer to 0.4–1.5/yr. A mixture (about 30% at ≈1.5/yr and 70% at ≈0.4/yr) is defensible as a source-based alternative, but it is my inference.
- **"25% never":** no source measures a "never" share directly. Proxies: 46% of critical edge KEVs not fully remediated within the DBIR period; 25% of OT advisories with no patch or mitigation; 27% of OT flaws triaged "Never". For IT, 25% stays an assumption. For OT, ≥25% is supported by the share without a vendor patch.
- **Industrial at a fifth of the pace (0.3/yr, a median of about 2.3 years):** this is consistent in direction with Dragos (71% wait for a maintenance cycle, often yearly or longer) and Claroty (12% of OT devices carry a KEV). No measured OT time-to-patch distribution exists in these sources, so this stays an assumption with supporting evidence.
- **Inside-network systems at half the pace:** I found no measurement. The Bitsight and DBIR data come from internet-facing or scanned assets.
- **Exploitation speed:** the median one-day becomes usable in public within about 2–6 weeks, and on edge devices at day 0. For SC45, one-day availability after disclosure is effectively immediate on the model's time step (years). Patching, not attacker weaponization, sets the one-day window.

### Gaps
- I found no OT patch-time distribution (only advisory shares and a prevalence snapshot), and no internal-network or IT patch-time data.
- The DBIR 2026 remediation figures (secondary sources report a 43-day median and 26% fully remediated) were not verified against the primary source.
- A secondary claim (VulnCheck-based) that 42.5% of the 2025 edge flaws affected end-of-life devices, which can never get a patch, could not be traced to a primary URL.
- I did not find a Mandiant time-to-exploit analysis for 2024 or 2025 other than the M-Trends −7-day mean.

## Q5. How often does an exposed operation burn the actor's capabilities, and its other accesses or tools?

### Takeaway
Once a zero-day is caught in use, it is patched (all zero-days in GTIG's 2025 set have patches), and other actors then use it widely as a one-day within days. Burns of a whole tool set or access network happen (leaks, vendor exposés, government takedowns), but I found no public rate for "share of other accesses lost after an exposure". The burn of the capability is well supported. The loss of accesses that share it (SC45's 2%) has no public basis.

### Cited Findings
- **Detection leads to patch:** "all zero-days included in our 2025 dataset have patches available." Figure date: Mar 2026. Quality A− (true by GTIG's inclusion rule). Model use: an exposed zero-day is burned for the patched target population. — [GTIG 2025 review](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/)
- **After the burn, wide reuse:** after disclosure, attacks with a former zero-day rose by up to five orders of magnitude, mostly within 30 days (2008–2011 data). Figure date: 2012. Quality B. — [Threatpost (Oct 2012)](https://threatpost.com/zero-day-attacks-thrive-months-disclosure-101612/77118)
- **After the burn, more actors:** in the 2021 Exchange case, at least five more clusters started within 1–3 days after the Mar 2 patch, and more than 5,000 servers had webshells. Figure date: Mar 10, 2021. Quality B. — [ESET (Mar 10, 2021)](https://www.welivesecurity.com/2021/03/10/exchange-servers-under-siege-10-apt-groups/)
- **Burned CSV exploits reused by an APT as n-days** about two months after the patch (2024). Figure date: Aug 2024. Quality B. — [Google TAG (Aug 29, 2024)](https://blog.google/threat-analysis-group/state-backed-attackers-and-commercial-surveillance-vendors-repeatedly-use-the-same-exploits/)
- **A leak burned a whole tool set:** the 2017 leak of an espionage tool set, followed within about 1–2.5 months by disruptive and criminal use of one of its exploits (WannaCry, NotPetya). Figure date: 2017. Quality C. — [AVG explainer](https://avg.com/en/signal/eternal-blue)
- **Exposure speed for espionage:** the median dwell time of detected cyber-espionage intrusions in 2025 was 122 days, and 52% of all intrusions were detected internally. Figure date: Apr 2026. Quality A− (only detected intrusions; survivorship bias). Model use: the hazard that an espionage operation is exposed. — [M-Trends 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/m-trends-2026)
- **OT burns are slower:** Dragos waited "90+ days for public disclosure" of actively exploited OT flaws. Figure date: Feb 2026. Quality B. Model use: the delay between exposure and patch in the industrial class. — [Dragos 2026 Year in Review blog](https://www.dragos.com/blog/dragos-2026-ot-cybersecurity-year-in-review)
- **The holder also retires capabilities:** RAND's life-status categories include "Killed by BUSBY", "Publicly Shared", "Security Patch" and code refactors. Not every loss of a capability comes from an exposure. The slide text did not give the shares. Figure date: 2017. Quality A− (categories only). — [Ablon & Bogart slides (Jul 2017)](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Exposure burns the capabilities" is supported for the exposed flaw. The burned flaw then becomes a one-day for many actors (Exchange 2021: at least five more clusters within 1–3 days; Bilge & Dumitras: up to 10^5× more attacks). If SC45 lets other actors pick up burned capabilities, these cases support near-immediate pickup.
- The exposure hazards are: espionage median dwell 122 days → ln2/(122/365) ≈ 2.1/yr among detected intrusions, and a zero-day in use lasting 312 days on average → ≈ 1.2/yr. Both are conditional on detection, so they do not give the share of operations never exposed. SC45's "80% of attacks exposed" has no direct public base rate. Disruptive effects are visible by nature, which supports a high value for attacks.
- **Loss of other accesses after a burn (SC45's 2%):** the public record (exposés, leaks, takedowns) shows that such losses happen, but no source gives a fraction. The 2% stays an unreviewed assumption.

### Gaps
- I found no coarse rate for "an exposed operation causes the loss of the actor's other accesses or tools". I did not search the government takedown cases (implant network disruptions) for counts in this pass.
- I found no public data on how often a burned capability is replaced from the same flaw family. The 2022 variant share (more than 40%) is only a proxy.

## Q6. What the figures imply for each current SC45 capability input

### Takeaway
Two inputs have a direct public basis: the defender patch rate for KEV flaws (1.45/yr from Bitsight ≈ SC45's 1.5/yr) and the held-flaw loss rate (RAND). For the second, SC45's 14.5% matches RAND's all-route disclosure hazard (1/6.9 years), not RAND's independent-rediscovery rate (5.7%/yr). The sharing, reuse, exposure, access-loss and new-system inputs have only case-level evidence and should stay labeled as unreviewed assumptions, with the bounds below.

### Cited Findings
- **Held flaw lost at 14.5%/yr.** Evidence: RAND collision 5.7%/yr (3.5–5.9%/yr, depending on the interval); RAND mean life 6.9 years → 14.5%/yr; Herr et al. 10.8–21.9% (Chrome, Android). — [Ablon & Bogart (2017)](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf); [Belfer (2017)](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/taking-stock-estimating-vulnerability-rediscovery)
