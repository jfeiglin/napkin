## 1. Multi-target state operations: share of targets with the intended effect, joint success or failure, and the shared cause

### Takeaway
Inside one operation, targets that share one access path, one tool or one management plane succeed or fail almost together (correlation near 1). When the shared step works, nearly all of them fall: 3 of 3 Ukrainian utilities within 30 minutes in 2015, most active KA-SAT modems in Ukraine in 2022, about 80,000 Stryker devices in 3 hours in 2026, and the 30 or more Polish renewable sites in 2025. When the shared step fails, they all fail: the 2016 relay stage through one code error, the Polish heat plant where EDR stopped the wiper on more than 100 machines at once. Outcomes differ mainly *between* groups of targets with different defenses inside the same operation (Poland: the farms were hit, the heat plant was not), or with different local conditions (Iran 2022: night power cuts limited damage at the steel plants). The evidence therefore points to a **bimodal** within-attack correlation. It is about 0.8–1.0 among targets that share the decisive step and low among targets each reached in its own way. The model's default of 0.3 is a middle value that no single case shows. The range's upper bound of 0.8 is too low for single-access operations.

### Cited Findings
**Ukraine, 23 December 2015 (three distribution utilities)**
- **3 of 3 targeted distribution companies lost power to customers; about 225,000 customers; the attacks came within 30 minutes of each other.** The attackers also intruded on three other organizations, some in other sectors, with no operational effect. Quotes: "remote cyber intrusions at three regional electric power distribution companies (Oblenergos) impacting approximately 225,000 customers"; "the cyber-attacks at each company occurred within 30 minutes of each other"; the other organizations "did not experience operational impacts." Shared methods: KillDisk wiping, corrupted firmware on serial-to-Ethernet devices, scheduled UPS disconnects, and breakers operated remotely "by multiple external humans". The role of BlackEnergy is unknown. — [CISA ICS alert IR-ALERT-H-16-056-01](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/ics-alerts/ir-alert-h-16-056-01) (about 25 Feb 2016, revised 20 Jul 2021; P-extract; M; D; pre-2020). → Model: within_correlation is near 1 among the 3 planned grid targets, which shared one team, one method and one time window. The three intruded but unaffected organizations show a 3/6 share only if they were intended effect targets, and the alert does not say that.

**Industroyer/CRASHOVERRIDE, 17 December 2016 (one transmission substation near Kyiv)**
- **The outage was achieved (power back "after just over an hour"). The intended protection-relay attack failed** because of "errors in the DoS tool code", and the attackers "failed to compromise as many control systems at the target as they intended." Dragos: if it had worked as intended, "the potential outage would have been more widespread than 2015" and "the duration of the outage may have stretched to months or longer." — [SecurityWeek on Dragos' reassessment](https://www.securityweek.com/russian-hackers-behind-ukraine-power-outage-may-have-sought-more-damage) (13 Sep 2019; S-read; the Dragos white paper itself was not opened; E; D; pre-2020). → Model: one tool flaw made one stage fail at every relay it touched. Stages that share a tool fail together.

**Viasat KA-SAT / AcidRain, 24 February 2022**
- **"tens of thousands of modems that were previously online and active dropped off the network", including "a majority of the previously active modems within Ukraine"**; "several thousand customers located in Ukraine" and "tens of thousands of other fixed broadband customers across Europe". Entry was through "a misconfiguration in a VPN appliance" into the management segment. Viasat then shipped "nearly 30,000 modems to distributors"; the network was "largely stabilized within hours and fully stabilized within several days." — [Viasat, KA-SAT network cyber attack overview](https://www.viasat.com/perspectives/corporate/2022/ka-sat-network-cyber-attack-overview/) (30 Mar 2022; P-extract; M, the victim's statement; D). → Model: one command through one management plane gives near-1 correlation across the modems it reached. The count of affected devices is "tens of thousands"; the share is a "majority" in Ukraine.
- Bateman judges Viasat "the only case where public evidence suggests much plausible military impact" among Russia's wartime cyber attacks. — [Bateman, Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2022/12/russias-wartime-cyber-operations-in-ukraine-military-impacts-influences-and-implications) (16 Dec 2022; P-extract; O; D).

**Industroyer2, April 2022 (one energy company, high-voltage substations)**
- The attackers "made an attempt to deploy the Industroyer2 malware against high-voltage electrical substations in Ukraine". The destructive actions were "scheduled for 2022-04-08", and the sample "contains eight different IP addresses of devices". CaddyWiper (Windows), ORCSHRED/AWFULSHRED (Linux) and SOLOSHRED (Solaris) were staged with it. ESET's article does not state the outcome. — [ESET, Industroyer2: Industroyer reloaded](https://www.welivesecurity.com/2022/04/12/industroyer2-industroyer-reloaded/) (12 Apr 2022; P-extract; M; D).
- Bateman counts Russia's known wartime ICS attempts as "two failed industrial control disruptions" (April and July 2022). He cites the headline "Ukraine Says It Thwarted a Sophisticated Russian Cyberattack on Its Power Grid" as Ukraine's claim. — [Bateman, Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2022/12/russias-wartime-cyber-operations-in-ukraine-military-impacts-influences-and-implications) (16 Dec 2022; P-extract; C/E; D). → Model: 0 of 1 planned effect, on Ukraine's claim.

**Sandworm against a Ukrainian substation operator, 10 and 12 October 2022**
- The attacker used OT living-off-the-land techniques "to likely trip the victim's substation circuit breakers, causing an unplanned power outage that coincided with mass missile strikes on critical infrastructure across Ukraine." Access started "on, or prior to, June 2022". The OT part "may have been developed in as little as two months", and the capability was possibly ready "as early as three weeks prior". The CaddyWiper deployed two days later "was limited to the victim's IT environment and did not impact the hypervisor or the SCADA virtual machine… This could indicate a lack of coordination across different individuals or operational subteams." Mandiant: "we lack sufficient evidence to assess a possible link" to the missile strikes. — [Mandiant/Google Cloud, Sandworm disrupts power in Ukraine](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/sandworm-disrupts-power-ukraine-operational-technology) (9 Nov 2023; P-extract; M/E; D). → Model: within one target, the effect stage succeeded and the follow-on wiper stage partly failed, so stages are not perfectly correlated even inside one target. Per-stage times: access to effect about 4 months; OT build about 2 months.

**Kyivstar, 12 December 2023 (one operator)**
- The SBU's Illia Vitiuk: the attack wiped "thousands of virtual servers and computers"; attackers were "in the system at least since May 2023" and probably had full access "at least since November"; it "did not significantly disrupt military communications". Most of Kyivstar's "25 million mobile and home internet subscribers" lost internet access. Attacker claim (Solntsepek): "We destroyed 10 thousand computers, more than 4 thousand servers, all cloud storage and backup systems." — [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/russian-hackers-wiped-thousands-of-systems-in-kyivstar-attack/) (4 Jan 2024; S-read; C by the victim state and by the attacker; D).
- Kyivstar disputed the destruction claims (13 December Facebook post). Reported scope: about 24 million users for days from 12 December. Kyivstar's CEO said on 20 December that all services were restored. — [Moscow Times/Reuters](https://themoscowtimes.com/2024/01/04/ukraine-says-russian-hackers-breached-telecom-giant-months-ahead-of-cyberattack-reuters-a83638) (4 Jan 2024; S-excerpt; C). → Model: one target. Damage was correlated across the systems that shared the core, while separate military networks with other protocols were spared (a shared-boundary effect). Access to effect was at least 7 months.

**Poland, 29 December 2025 (30+ wind and solar farms, one heat-and-power plant, one manufacturer; one actor)**
- **Targets and outcome at the farms:** "an attack targeted at least 30 wind and solar farms… The attack resulted in a loss of communication between the facilities and distribution system operators (DSOs), but it did not affect ongoing electricity generation." The report also says "the combined loss of capacity across all 30 facilities would not have affected the stability of the Polish power system". It does not give separate counts of farms targeted and farms damaged. — [CERT Polska, Energy Sector Incident Report](https://cert.pl/uploads/docs/CERT_Polska_Energy_Sector_Incident_Report_2025.pdf) (30 Jan 2026; P-full; M; D).
- **Shared cause of joint success:** "In each affected facility, a FortiGate device was present… In every case, the VPN interface was exposed to the Internet and allowed authentication to accounts defined in the configuration without multi-factor authentication." "It is a common practice in the industry to reuse the same accounts and passwords across multiple facilities." Hitachi RTU560 units were "configured with default credentials, including an account named 'Default.'" "Each of the affected facilities used Moxa NPort 6xxx serial device servers… configured with default login credentials… all Moxa devices accessible at the facility were targeted." "None of the devices that supported secure update had this feature enabled." — [CERT Polska report](https://cert.pl/uploads/docs/CERT_Polska_Energy_Sector_Incident_Report_2025.pdf) (P-full; M; D).
- **Automated plan with a stop-on-failure rule:** "The activity observed within individual substations appears to have been at least partially automated. Devices were damaged in ascending order of IP addressing. It was observed that if the attack failed at a given IP address within a network segment, it was not continued against subsequent addresses". Relays were attacked "in two cases". — [CERT Polska report](https://cert.pl/uploads/docs/CERT_Polska_Energy_Sector_Incident_Report_2025.pdf) (P-full; M; D).
- **Heat-and-power plant (joint failure):** months of access and privileged Active Directory accounts, then a wiper pushed by group policy. "The executable file was not detected by antivirus software; however, its execution was blocked at runtime by the EDR solution through the use of a canary mechanism… This resulted in the halting of data overwriting on more than 100 machines on which the file had already been executed. On the same day, the attacker made another attempt to execute a slightly modified version of the wiper, but this attempt was also unsuccessful." The web summary says the attack "did not achieve the attacker's intended effect of disrupting heat supply to end users." — [CERT Polska report](https://cert.pl/uploads/docs/CERT_Polska_Energy_Sector_Incident_Report_2025.pdf) (P-full) and [CERT Polska web summary](https://cert.pl/en/posts/2026/01/incident-report-energy-sector-2025/) (P-extract); M; D.
- **Manufacturer:** "the target was opportunistic in nature and not linked to the other affected organizations". The way in was a Fortinet device whose "configuration had been stolen and publicly disclosed"; LazyWiper was pushed by group policy. The report text I read does not state the outcome. **Context:** the attacks coincided with "low temperatures and snowstorms… shortly before New Year's Eve". Attribution: the infrastructure overlaps with "Static Tundra" (Berserk Bear / Ghost Blizzard / Dragonfly), "the first publicly described destructive activity attributed to this cluster." — [CERT Polska report](https://cert.pl/uploads/docs/CERT_Polska_Energy_Sector_Incident_Report_2025.pdf) (P-full; M/E; D). → Model: correlation is near 1 within the farm set (one vector, one automated plan) and near 1 within the plant (one EDR). Across target types in one operation the outcomes differed. The unit of correlation is the shared access path or the shared defense, not the operation.

**Stryker, 11 March 2026 (one firm, devices wiped through its own Intune tenant)**
- About **80,000 devices** were wiped, per "a source familiar". Handala claimed "over 200,000 systems, servers, and mobile devices" (unverified). - **A structural reading of within-attack correlation (my derivation).** Suppose each target needs one shared step (access path, management plane or tool) that works with probability q, and then one independent step per target that works with probability r. The pairwise correlation is then ρ_w = r(1−q)/(1−qr). Examples: q=0.7, r=0.9 → 0.73; q=0.5, r=0.9 → 0.82; q=0.9, r=0.9 → 0.47; q=0.7, r=0.5 → 0.23; q=0.9, r=0.5 → 0.09. The model's default of 0.3 corresponds to q≈0.7, r≈0.55. The correlation is high when the shared step is uncertain and the per-target steps are reliable, which is the pattern in every case above. This argues for building the kill chain stage by stage, with each stage marked as shared across the targets of an attack or done per target. ρ_w then follows from the stages instead of being set as a constant.
- **Mapping to within_correlation (default 0.3, range 0–0.8):** the evidence **contradicts the upper bound** for operations through one access or one command (observed about 0.8–1.0). It **supports low values** only where targets are "each hit in its own way", and I found no state case of that. The default is **not contradicted, but no case shows it**: it is a mixture. Proposal: widen the range to 0–1, or make ρ_w depend on the share of the chain that is shared, which falls as AI tailors each target.
- **Mapping to targets_now (default 1, range 1–3):** capable states already hit more than 3 organizations or facilities per operation without evident AI. Examples: 3 utilities in 2015, at least 30 facilities in one day in 2025, and 22 organizations in the first week of 2022 (Q2). The range upper bound is **contradicted** if a target means an organization or facility. It **holds** if a target means one independent access, because most cases above used one shared access. The model needs an explicit definition of "target".
- **Mapping to campaign_time:** the multi-target operations above ran in 30 minutes to about 3 hours (Ukraine 2015, Stryker) or in one day (Poland). Access was built over months beforehand (Kyivstar ≥7 months, October 2022 ~4 months, the Polish plant months).

## 2. Across the attacks of one campaign: Russia's destructive campaign against Ukraine, 2022–2025

### Takeaway
The campaign was front-loaded and moved as one. In late February 2022 a salvo hit 22 organizations in one week (Microsoft). Nearly 40 destructive attacks followed in the 6.5 weeks to 8 April. The rate then fell to about 1 a week from mid-April to late June, to near zero in August–September, with a small spike in October. Impact fell in step, through causes shared by every attack: pre-positioned access was used up and "often lost as the attack was remediated"; Russia's "initially large stockpile of preplanned cyber fires" ran out; and one pooled defense (EDR detections pushed to all customers, backups, CERT-UA and vendor support) learned from each wave. By the second half of 2025 CERT-UA recorded zero critical incidents among 5,927 incidents for the year. No source gives outcomes attack by attack, so the between-attack correlation cannot be estimated directly. The visible co-movement is mostly a drift, in which defender adaptation and the depletion of the attacker's stock affect all attacks at once. The model already represents part of this drift through pooled alertness.

### Cited Findings
- **"two to three incidents a week since the eve of invasion. From February 23 to April 8, we saw evidence of nearly 40 discrete destructive attacks that permanently destroyed files in hundreds of systems across dozens of organizations in Ukraine."** "More than 40% of the destructive attacks were aimed at organizations in critical infrastructure sectors… Thirty-two percent of destructive incidents affected Ukrainian government organizations." "At least eight destructive malware families". "the threat actors are slightly modifying the malware to evade detection with each wave of deployment." — [Microsoft, Special Report: Ukraine](https://complexdiscovery.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Microsoft-Ukraine-Special-Report-April-2022.pdf) (27 Apr 2022; P-full, from a third-party copy because Microsoft's original link returned 404; part of the PDF text is font-encoded and I decoded it; Bateman quotes the same "permanently destroyed files…" phrase; M; D). → Model: shared wiper families and wave-by-wave retooling are shared causes of between-attack co-movement.
- **Pooled defense shown at work:** "customers running endpoint detection and response (EDR) solutions were able to respond to alerts and remediate intrusions before a destructive attack was launched"; controlled folder access "meaningfully mitigated some of the damage done by destructive wiper malware"; "high concentrations of malicious network activity frequently overlapped with high-intensity fighting during the first six-plus weeks of the invasion." — [Microsoft Special Report](https://complexdiscovery.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Microsoft-Ukraine-Special-Report-April-2022.pdf) (27 Apr 2022; P-full; M/E; D).
- **Weekly tempo (Microsoft and Mandiant via Bateman):** hundreds of systems were targeted the day before the invasion and "22 organizations were hit in the first week". Then "about three attacks per week" for five weeks, falling to "about one per week" by mid-April and holding through late June. "Little to no wiper activity" in August and September, then "a small October spike". Mandiant counted "five attacks in the first week and under one per week through October, with just one attack from June to September." By late June Microsoft reported "eight destructive malware programs used against 48 Ukrainian agencies and enterprises", and "roughly 50 Ukrainian organizations" by mid-October. Ukraine's SSSCIP reported "56 operations affecting the availability" of systems in the first four months. — [Bateman, Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2022/12/russias-wartime-cyber-operations-in-ukraine-military-impacts-influences-and-implications) (16 Dec 2022; P-extract; M compiled from vendors, E; D). Bateman warns his Figure 1 is "a very general picture of rough patterns and orders of magnitude" that "should not be understood as quantitatively precise."
- **Success over time (Bateman):** cyber fires "peaked in the days immediately before and after the invasion" and "subsequently declined in number, novelty, and impact". They "declined precipitously as Russian hackers proved unable to maintain such a high operational tempo". Early success depended on "its initially large stockpile of preplanned cyber fires". The decline in novel wipers "suggests a limited stockpile of technical resources". Russia possibly used wave tactics, having "halted destructive cyber attacks in the summer before resuming in October". The total was "just a few dozen data deletion operations" plus "two failed industrial control disruptions". Ukraine's cyber agency said attempted attacks were growing but "most of them are unsuccessful"; Ukraine reported "no data was lost because backups were available"; Mandiant saw a shift to "quick-and-dirty methods". Victim impact was "limited operational impact" per Microsoft. — [Bateman, Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2022/12/russias-wartime-cyber-operations-in-ukraine-military-impacts-influences-and-implications) (16 Dec 2022; P-extract; E/C; D).
- **Phases (Google TAG / Mandiant):** "Mandiant observed more destructive cyberattacks in Ukraine during the first four months of 2022 than in the previous eight years with attacks peaking around the start of the invasion." "the pace of attacks slowed and appeared less coordinated than the initial wave in February 2022." "Mandiant observed at least six unique wipers". "While the destructive cyberattacks did achieve significant widespread disruption initially in some Ukrainian networks, they were likely not as impactful as previous Russian cyberattacks in Ukraine. To conduct the initial waves of destructive activity, Russian actors often employed accesses gained months before, which were often lost as the attack was remediated." "Destructive attacks often occurred more quickly after the attacker gained or regained access, often via compromised edge infrastructure." Five phases: I, espionage and pre-positioning (2019–Jan 2022); II, initial destructive operations (Feb–Apr 2022); III, sustained targeting (May–Jul); IV, maintaining footholds (Aug–Sep); V, renewed disruptive attacks (Oct–Dec). The wider spillover experts expected "largely did not happen in 2022". Phishing: "a 250% increase targeting users in Ukraine and an over 300% increase targeting users in NATO countries — both compared to a 2020 baseline." — [Google, Fog of War](https://services.google.com/fh/files/blogs/google_fog_of_war_research_report.pdf) (Feb 2023; P-full; M/E; D). → Model: campaign waves last about 2–3 months, and access is used up by use.
- **Timing with missile strikes:** the October 2022 outage "coincided with mass missile strikes on critical infrastructure across Ukraine" (Mandiant, see Q1). Bateman finds most of Microsoft's cyber–kinetic pairings "uncompelling". The strongest case, the 11 March Dnipro attack, he calls "largely wasted efforts". For the 1 July DTEK case he raises a "spurious correlation". Microsoft's oblast-level match was 15 oblasts matched against 9 mismatched, which he calls too coarse. — [Bateman, Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2022/12/russias-wartime-cyber-operations-in-ukraine-military-impacts-influences-and-implications) (P-extract; O/E; D).
- **Espionage funnel (surveillance chain, outside Ukraine):** "Russian network intrusion efforts on 128 organizations in 42 countries outside Ukraine"; "the Russian targeting we've identified has been successful 29 percent of the time"; "A quarter of these successful intrusions has led to confirmed exfiltration of an organization's data." On defense in Ukraine: defenses "have proven stronger than offensive cyber capabilities", though "some destructive attacks have been successful". — [Microsoft, Defending Ukraine: Early Lessons](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2022/06/22/defending-ukraine-early-lessons-from-the-cyber-war/) (22 Jun 2022; P-extract; M; D). Per-stage arithmetic: about 37 successful intrusions, of which about 9 had confirmed exfiltration (≈7% of targeted organizations).
- **2025 levels:** CERT-UA handled 5,927 incidents in 2025, 37.4% more than the 4,315 of 2024 — [dev.ua](https://dev.ua/en/news/u-2025-rotsi-kilkist-kiberatak-zrosla-na-37-1767858414) (Jan 2026; S-excerpt; M). "In the second half of 2025, no critical cyber incidents were recorded in Ukraine"; high-level incidents fell 17%; malware distribution rose while actual infections fell. — [UNN, citing SSSCIP/CERT-UA](https://unn.ua/en/news/no-critical-cyber-incidents-recorded-in-ukraine-by-the-end-of-2025-state-special-communications-service) (page dated 3 Apr 2026; P-extract; M by the defender; D). CERT-UA says its statistics cover only incidents its staff detected and handled (S-excerpt).
- **2025 destructive activity:** Sandworm "deployed data wipers (ZEROLOT, Sting) against governmental entities", energy and logistics firms, and the grain sector, with the "likely objective being the weakening of the Ukrainian economy". No counts or outcomes are given. — [ESET APT Activity Report Q2–Q3 2025](https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/eset-research/eset-apt-activity-report-q2-2025-q3-2025/) (6 Nov 2025; P-extract; E; D).

### Inferences
- **Burst size of attack timing.** From Bateman's weekly figures I reconstructed an approximate weekly series for late February to October 2022 (35 weeks): 22, then 3 a week for 5 weeks, 1 a week for about 11 weeks, a few in July, 0 in August–September, about 3 in October. It has a mean of about 1.5 attacks a week and a variance-to-mean ratio of about 9. A steady (Poisson) campaign would give 1. This is a rough reconstruction, not a measurement. The campaign is strongly front-loaded, so a model that spreads ~235 attacks evenly over a year will understate how bunched the opening of a war is.
- **What moves campaign success together** (shared causes found): (1) a shared, finite stock of pre-positioned access and new tools, used up by use (Fog of War, Bateman); (2) one pooled defense: vendor detections pushed to every customer, EDR, controlled folder access, backups, CERT-UA (Microsoft, Bateman, UNN); (3) shared malware families retooled in waves (Microsoft); (4) shared timing with the kinetic campaign (Mandiant's October 2022 case; weak overall per Bateman). Causes (1) and (2) are drifts that act on all later attacks, which matches the model's pooled-alertness mechanism. In the model, between_correlation should therefore stand only for the residual shock *not* explained by alertness and stock depletion. Otherwise the same co-movement is counted twice. Section 4 gives the size of that residual in ransomware data.
- **Mapping to between_correlation (default 0.05, range 0–0.3):** the Ukraine data neither confirm nor reject 0.05, because there are no outcomes attack by attack. They show large co-movement over the campaign that comes mostly from drift. That **supports keeping the drift in the alertness and depletion mechanisms** and keeping the constant small.
- **Mapping to campaign_time (default 1 month, range 1 week–3 months):** **supported**. The salvo lasted about one week, waves and phases about 2–3 months, and access-to-effect cycles 2–7 months.
- **Defender alertness.** The evidence supports one pooled alertness: a detection learned from one victim is pushed to all, within days. Alertness rose with each *wave or technique* exposed (new wiper family, new access path), not only with the count of exposed attacks. Retooling each wave ("slightly modifying the malware… with each wave") partly resets it.
- **Noisy damage assessment.** Observers' first-week counts differ by a factor of about 4 (Microsoft: 22 organizations hit; Mandiant: 5 attacks; the units differ), and outcome claims are contested (Kyivstar). Battle damage assessment is "immature" (Bateman, Q6). This supports the model's noisy-assessment learning.

### Gaps
- I did not open: Microsoft Digital Defense Reports 2022–2025; later ESET reports giving counts by quarter; SSSCIP's full half-year analytical reports (counts of destructive incidents by group and outcome); the CyberPeace Institute's Ukraine platform; CSIS analyses. The search budget ran out. These are the places to look for destructive-attack counts per quarter for 2023–2025.
- No source found gives, per destructive attack, whether the intended effect was reached. A direct estimate of between-attack correlation inside the campaign is therefore not possible from public data.

## 3. Cyber-insurance and actuarial evidence: correlation within and across firms, systemic and common-mode events

### Takeaway
The only direct numeric estimates I found come from Böhme & Kataria (2006), using honeynet data from 2004–05, so they are pre-2020 and measure the arrival of automated attacks, not success. Global (between-firm) correlation had a median of **0.03** (0.00–0.12 by attack type). Internal (within-network) correlation was **0.00–0.105**. Common-mode events show rare, large shocks on top of that low baseline: CrowdStrike (July 2024) hit **25% of the Fortune 500** at once; WannaCry hit **at least 34% of NHS trusts in England**; NotPetya cost insurers **more than $3 bn**. Across firms the dependence is therefore a small constant plus rare jumps, not one middle-sized constant.

### Cited Findings
- **Twin-tier framework.** "At the first tier, is the correlation of cyber-risks within a firm i.e. correlated failure of multiple systems on its internal network. At second tier, is the correlation in risk at a global level i.e. correlation across independent firms in an insurer's portfolio." "insider attacks exhibit high internal but low global correlation". Conclusion: "cyber-insurance is best suited for classes of risk with high internal and low global correlation". — [Böhme & Kataria, WEIS 2006](http://www.econinfosec.org/archive/weis2006/docs/16.pdf) (June 2006; P-full; E; T; pre-2020). → Model: the tiers map directly onto within_correlation and between_correlation.
- **Global correlation (beta-binomial, daily attacked-sensor counts).** "we found evidence for the existence of global attack correlation in 19 out of 27 port sequences (≈ 70 %…)". "The overall amount of correlation remains very small with a median ρBB of 0.03. Single ports, however, yield point estimates for the correlation coefficient above 0.1, such as port 22 (ssh) and port 23 (telnet)." Table 3 gives: ssh 0.124 (95% CI 0.10–0.15); telnet 0.105; 1080 0.094; 4128 0.080; 445 0.057; 80 0.045; 139 0.002 (CI 0.00–0.02). Data: Leurré.com honeynet, 183,000 raw events from 35 sensors (Feb 2003–Sep 2005), reduced to 13 sensors on 4 continents, June 2004–Aug 2005, 457 daily points. The authors call the estimates "preliminary indications". — [Böhme & Kataria](http://www.econinfosec.org/archive/weis2006/docs/16.pdf) (P-full; M; T; pre-2020). → Model: between_correlation **supports the low end** (0.03; tail to 0.12). The default 0.05 is inside the observed spread and the upper bound of 0.3 is above it. Caution: this measures whether attacks *arrive* together across firms, not whether *outcomes* succeed together.
- **One-factor latent model.** "The median ρ0 = 0.18 is somewhat higher than the median for ρBB with single peaks range up to about 0.30". "ρ0 is not directly comparable to ρBB". PCA: "the first of them is absolutely dominant". — [Böhme & Kataria](http://www.econinfosec.org/archive/weis2006/docs/16.pdf) (P-full; M; T; pre-2020). → Model: a single dominant common factor drives the across-firm dependence.
- **Internal correlation (infections within a network).** Table 5, five domains: ρI = 0.0045, 0.1050, 0.0040, 0.0166, 0.0000. "Only five (out of 13) domains reported a considerable number of attacks from within their own network". "the size of internal correlation differs significantly between networks". "our estimates for ρI and π are only indicative". The authors flag domains 2 (the 0.105 value) and 5 as "visibly prone" to errors in estimating network size. — [Böhme & Kataria](http://www.econinfosec.org/archive/weis2006/docs/16.pdf) (P-full; M; T; pre-2020). → Model: worm spread inside a network shows *low* within correlation, very unlike the single-command state operations in Q1. Correlation depends on the mechanism.
- **CrowdStrike outage, 19 July 2024.** "A quarter of the Fortune 500 was impacted (125 corporations)". Direct loss $5.4 bn (Fortune 500 excluding Microsoft); healthcare $1.938 bn; "The portion of the loss covered under cyber insurance policies is likely to be no more than 10% to 20%." — [Parametrix](https://parametrixinsurance.com/in-the-news/crowdstrike-to-cost-fortune-500-5-4-billion-insured-loss-range-of-540-million-to-1-08-billion) (24 Jul 2024; P-extract; E; T). CyberCube put insured losses at $0.4–1.5 bn, possibly the largest single insured loss event in about 20 years of affirmative cyber cover — [Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2024/07/25/785484.htm) (25 Jul 2024; S-excerpt; E). Guy Carpenter put them at $0.3–1 bn — [Reinsurance News](https://www.reinsurancene.ws/crowdstrike-event-insured-loss-to-fall-between-300m-1bn-guy-carpenter/) (Aug 2024; S-excerpt; E). → Model: one shared vendor dependency put 25% of large firms down on the same day. That is a common-mode jump, not a small constant.
- **WannaCry and the NHS, May 2017.** "at least 81 of 236 trusts" in England were affected ("at least 34%"); 603 primary-care and other organizations were infected, including 595 GP practices. "all organisations infected by WannaCry shared the same vulnerability." The NHS was not specifically targeted (WannaCry "was released worldwide"). A "kill switch" stopped it. — [National Audit Office](https://www.nao.org.uk/reports/investigation-wannacry-cyber-attack-and-the-nhs/) (27 Oct 2017; P-extract; M; T; pre-2020). → Model: in an untargeted spread through one flaw, about a third of a sector's organizations were hit together. Two shared causes decided the outcome together: the same unpatched flaw (success) and one kill switch (failure).
- **NotPetya:** insured losses above $3 bn, mostly through "silent" cover (see Q1; S-excerpt).

### Inferences
- **The baseline and the jump are different things.** On ordinary days, attacks arriving at different firms are nearly independent (ρ ≈ 0.03). The tail is made of rare shared-dependency events that hit 25–35% of a population at once (CrowdStrike, WannaCry). For the model, a constant between_correlation of 0.05 roughly captures the baseline but not the jumps. For an attacker, the jumps cut both ways. A shared flaw can make many attacks succeed together (WannaCry, MOVEit). One shared defense can make many fail together (the kill switch, an EDR signature, the Polish plant's EDR).
- **Direction of the evidence for the "casino" argument:** low baseline correlation across independent targets *supports* aggregation. The heavy tail of common shocks, and the drift in Q4, *undercut* the claim that the totals are predictable.

### Gaps
- Not reached (search budget exhausted): Eling & Jung (2018) copula estimates of the dependence of data-breach losses across sectors; Bessy-Roland, Boumezoued & Hillairet (2021) multivariate Hawkes contagion; Chen, Kataria & Krishnan (2011) on correlated failures and diversification; Geer et al. (2003) on monoculture; Lloyd's and CyberCube systemic scenarios. Numeric correlations from these could not be added.
- No study found that measures correlation of attack *success* (not arrival) across firms.

## 4. Ransomware as the closest working case of many attacks on unrelated organizations

### Takeaway
Per-stage success rates exist and they drift a lot over years, through causes shared by the whole victim population. Two examples: the share of attacks that encrypted data fell from **76% to 50%** in Sophos' 2023–2025 reports, and the share of victims who paid fell from **85% in 2019 to about 20% in Q4 2025** in Coveware's data. Measured against sampling error, the short-run (quarter-to-quarter) common shock in 2024–2026 is **close to zero** (implied correlation ≤ ~0.003). The year-scale common movement, which includes the drift, implies a correlation of about **0.05–0.08**. Mass zero-day extortion campaigns are the nearest real case of "hundreds of unrelated organizations hit at the same moment". The share of victims who paid varied from campaign to campaign **25% → 20% → 2.5% → 0%** (implied correlation inside a campaign about **0.11–0.14**). Coveware's own reading: hitting hundreds of victims through one flaw no longer guarantees hundreds of payments. The common movement comes from backups and EDR, shared legal and incident-response advice, takedowns, and disclosure rules.

### Cited Findings
- **Sophos 2025 (3,400 respondents in 17 countries, organizations hit in the last year).** Data encrypted in 50% of attacks, the lowest in six years. 44% of attacks were stopped before encryption, a six-year high. — [Sophos press release](https://www.sophos.com/zh-cn/press/press-releases/2025/06/nearly-half-companies-opt-pay-ransom-sophos-report-finds) and [TechEconomy summary](https://techeconomy.ng/10-key-findings-from-sophos-state-of-ransomware-report-2025) (June 2025; S-excerpt; M from a survey; T). "Just under half (49%) paid the ransom and got their data back… a small reduction from last year's 56%"; this is "the second highest rate of ransom payments in the last six years". Root causes: exploited vulnerabilities in 32% of attacks; compromised credentials "dropped from 29% in 2024 to 23% in 2025". — [Sophos blog, State of Ransomware 2025](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/blog/the-state-of-ransomware-2025/) (24 Jun 2025; P-extract; M; T).
- **Sophos 2024 (5,000 respondents in 14 countries, fieldwork Jan–Feb 2024).** The attack rate was 59%, down from 66% "reported in both the previous two years". 56% of victims with encrypted data paid. 68% used backups (70% the year before). Median payment $2 m against $400,000 (n = 1,097 payers). **Within one organization:** "On average, 49% of an organization's computers were impacted. Only 4% reported that 91% or more of their devices were affected." — [Sophos blog, State of Ransomware 2024](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/blog/the-state-of-ransomware-2024/) (30 Apr 2024; P-extract; M; T). The global encryption rate for the 2024 report was 70% — [Sophos retail 2024 report](https://www.sophos.com/ja-jp/blog/the-state-of-ransomware-in-retail-2024) (S-excerpt).
- **Sophos 2023:** "In 76 percent of ransomware attacks against surveyed organizations, cyber criminals actually succeeded in encrypting data", described as the "highest level in four years". — [Sophos press release](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/press/press-releases/2023/05/data-encryption-ransomware-reaches-highest-level-in-four-years) (10 May 2023; S-excerpt; M; T).
- **Coveware share of victims who paid (cases Coveware handled):** 85% in 2019 when tracking began — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/01/31/ransomware_payment_rates_drop/) (31 Jan 2024; S-excerpt; pre-2020 value). "Only 37% of ransomware victims paid a ransom in Q4 [2022], a record low as security and backup continuity investments pay off" — [Coveware report index](https://coveware.com/ransomware-quarterly-reports) (P-extract). Q4 2024: "an all-time low of 25%" — [Coveware Q4 2024](https://coveware.com/2025/02/q4-report/) (4 Feb 2025; P-extract). Q1 and Q2 2025: 26% — [Business Wire release](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250812436558/en) (Aug 2025; S-excerpt). Q3 2025: "fell to a historical low of 23%", and data-exfiltration-only cases 19% — [Coveware Q3 2025](https://coveware.com/2025/10/insider-threats-loom-while-ransom-payment-rates-plummet/) (24 Oct 2025; P-extract). Q4 2025: "approximately 20%", after rates "previously oscillated in the 25–35% range" — [Coveware, Feb 2026](https://coveware.com/2026/02/why-zero-day-downstream-mass-data-extortion-campaigns-are-losing-their-bite/) (3 Feb 2026; P-extract). Q1 2026: "ticked up slightly to 23%" — [Coveware, Apr 2026](https://coveware.com/2026/04/patch-management-goes-from-hard-to-ludicrous-in-the-agentic-ai-era/) (30 Apr 2026; P-extract). Q2 2026: overall rate a "new record low" (no number in the text); exfiltration-only 15% — [Coveware, Jul 2026](https://coveware.com/2026/07/adverse-cyber-extortions-are-more-common-than-commonly-advised/) (29 Jul 2026; P-extract). All M from Coveware's own cases; T. Coveware gives no sample sizes, and its quarterly charts are images I could not read.

Exfiltration-only declines reflect "the growing maturation of both enterprises and the vendors that support cyber incidents, most notably specialized privacy attorneys" — [Q3 2025](https://coveware.com/2025/10/insider-threats-loom-while-ransom-payment-rates-plummet/). New disclosure rules — [index](https://coveware.com/ransomware-quarterly-reports). (P-extract; O/E; T.)
- **Mass zero-day "downstream" extortion campaigns (one flaw, many unrelated victims at once, CL0P):** Accellion (Q1 2021), "100+" organizations, payment rate "close to 25%". - **Chainalysis, whole-ecosystem totals:** "about $820 million in 2025, roughly 8 percent less than the year before"; claimed victims up 50% year on year (eCrime.ch); "More than 8,000 organizations were publicly named on leak sites last year"; "the share of victims paying dropped to an all-time low of 28 percent"; the median ransom *demand* rose "from $12,738 in 2024 to $59,556 in 2025". Old groups "have been raided, sanctioned, arrested, or simply popped back up under new logos". — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/27/ransomware_chainalysis/) (27 Feb 2026; S-read; M/E; T). The 2024 total was revised to $892 m, and Chainalysis expects 2025 to "approach or exceed $900 million" as more payments are traced — [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/ransomware-attacks-rose-50-in-2025-chainalysis) (Feb 2026; S-excerpt).
- **Payment did not always buy the promised result:** after the LockBit takedown it emerged the group kept stolen data despite deletion promises, so "victims were paying for a result that was never actually delivered". Coveware gives no frequency. — [Coveware, Jul 2026](https://coveware.com/2026/07/adverse-cyber-extortions-are-more-common-than-commonly-advised/) (P-extract; O; T).

### Inferences (my calculations; inputs as cited above)
- **Year-scale common factor (Sophos encryption share).** For the 2023, 2024 and 2025 reports (76%, 70%, 50%), with about 2,000–3,400 victims per year, the sampling variance is about 0.0001. The observed between-year variance is 0.012–0.019. The implied pairwise correlation of two victims' outcomes in the same year is excess variance divided by p(1−p): **≈0.05–0.08**. Adding the unverified 2020 value of 73% gives 0.05–0.06. This includes the downward drift and changes in survey mix, so it is an upper bound on random yearly shocks. → between_correlation: the default 0.05 is **supported** as a year-scale value that includes drift. The range upper bound of 0.3 is far above it.
- **Quarter-scale common factor (Coveware payment share, Q4 2024–Q1 2026: 25, 26, 26, 23, 20, 23%).** The raw variance is 0.00054 and the detrended residual variance 0.00033, with a trend of −0.9 points per quarter. Sampling variance would be 0.00061 for 300 cases a quarter and 0.00018 for 1,000 cases. The implied short-run correlation is therefore **≈0 to 0.003**: quarter-to-quarter wobble is about what sampling alone would give. The large movement is the slow drift from 85% to 20% over six years. → Model: short-run shocks common to unrelated attacks are tiny. The common movement is a slow, population-wide drift, which is defender adaptation, not noise. This **narrows** between_correlation, as a pure shock term net of modeled alertness, toward 0–0.01. Coveware's case counts are not published, so this rests on assumed n.
- **Within-campaign common factor (mass zero-day campaigns: 25%, 20%, 2.5%, 0%).** With 100–3,000 victims per campaign, sampling variance is negligible (~0.0006). The between-campaign variance is 0.012–0.016 around a mean of 0.12. The implied correlation of two victims' payment outcomes inside one campaign is **≈0.11–0.14**. This is a monetization-stage outcome; the access stage (data stolen) was near-uniform across vulnerable victims. → within_correlation: a case of many organizations through one flaw gives **≈0.1–0.15 at the final stage**, below the default of 0.3. The ordered decline from campaign to campaign shows pooled learning across all potential victims. That is direct support for the model's pooled alertness, in a criminal setting.
- **Is the attacker "the casino"?** Partly. Whole-ecosystem revenue was fairly stable (about −8% in 2025 while claimed victims rose 50%), so aggregation does smooth totals. But the yield per victim fell steadily through causes shared by all victims: backups and EDR, legal and incident-response advice, takedowns and disclosure rules. The house edge itself drifts, and it drifts against the attacker as exposure grows. Aggregation removes noise in the short run; it does not remove drift caused by adaptation.
- **Per-stage numbers for an effects chain (ransomware analog):** access to encryption attempt: 44% stopped before encryption (Sophos 2025); encryption achieved: 50% of attacks (2025); within an organization, a mean of 49% of computers hit and 4% with more than 90% (Sophos 2024); monetization: 20–28% of victims paid in 2025 (Coveware, Chainalysis).

### Gaps
- Coveware does not publish its case counts per quarter, and its full quarterly series exists only as chart images. The sampling-error comparison rests on assumed n of 300–3,000.
- The global encryption shares in Sophos' 2021 and 2022 reports were not verified (often quoted as 54% and 65%) and are not used. The 2020 value of 73% comes only from a search excerpt.
- Chainalysis' 2023 total and its quarterly series were not checked.

## 5. AI-tailored attacks against many organizations: what share of targets was hit?

### Takeaway
There is one usable count. Anthropic's GTG-1002 espionage campaign (September 2025) targeted "roughly 30 entities", and Anthropic "validated a handful of successful intrusions". That is about 10–17% if "handful" means 3–5, with the AI doing 80–90% of tactical work in parallel across targets. The AI also "frequently overstated findings", which adds noise to the attacker's own damage assessment. The extortion case (August 2025) names at least 17 organizations targeted but no success share. Google's November 2025 report gives no victim counts or success rates. In Poland, an LLM-written wiper was slower in practice than intended. No data yet show AI-tailored attacks on many organizations giving a predictable share of successes.

### Cited Findings
- **GTG-1002 (Chinese state-sponsored; espionage):** "The operation targeted roughly 30 entities and our investigation validated a handful of successful intrusions." "the threat actor able to leverage AI to execute 80-90% of tactical operations independently at physically impossible request rates." Limitation: "Claude frequently overstated findings and occasionally fabricated data during autonomous operations, claiming to have obtained credentials that didn't work or identifying critical discoveries that proved to be publicly available information… requiring careful validation of all claimed results." Parallel work: reconnaissance "occurred simultaneously across multiple targets, with the AI maintaining separate operational contexts for each active campaign". **Per-phase times:** vulnerability discovery and validation took "Claude's autonomous actions (1-4 hours)" against "Human operator actions (2-10 minutes)"; database extraction took "(2-6 hours)" against "(5-20 minutes)". The phases were initialization and target selection, reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, credential harvesting and lateral movement, data collection, and documentation. Detected "in mid-September 2025". — [Anthropic full report](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/ec212e6566a0d47/original/Disrupting-the-first-reported-AI-orchestrated-cyber-espionage-campaign.pdf) (Nov 2025; P-full; M/E by the AI provider; D, because the actor is a state). The summary page adds "perhaps 4-6 critical decision points per hacking campaign" and "thousands of requests, often multiple per second" (corrected on 14 Nov 2025) — [Anthropic news](https://www.anthropic.com/news/disrupting-AI-espionage) (13 Nov 2025; P-extract). Some security experts doubt the 80–90% figure — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/anthropic-reports-the-first-80-90-percent-ai-orchestrated-cyber-espionage-campaign-but-cybersecurity-critics-are-sceptical) (Nov 2025; S-excerpt; O).
- **Data extortion with Claude Code (criminal):** the actor "targeted at least 17 distinct organizations, including in healthcare, the emergency services", government and religious institutions. "Claude Code was used to automate reconnaissance, harvesting victims' credentials, and penetrating networks"; "Claude was allowed to make both tactical and strategic decisions, such as deciding which data to exfiltrate"; it "analyzed the exfiltrated financial data to determine appropriate ransom amounts". Ransoms "sometimes exceeded $500,000". The page does not say how many of the 17 were breached or paid. — [Anthropic, August 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/detecting-countering-misuse-aug-2025) (27 Aug 2025; P-extract; M/E; T).
- **Google GTIG AI Threat Tracker:** PROMPTSTEAL, used by APT28 against Ukraine, is "our first observation of malware querying an LLM deployed in live operations". PROMPTFLUX "does not demonstrate an ability to compromise a victim network or device"; PROMPTLOCK is a "proof of concept". Malware that rewrites itself "on an hourly basis to evade detection" is "still nascent". No victim counts or success rates are given. — [Google GTIG](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/threat-actor-usage-of-ai-tools) (5 Nov 2025; P-extract; M/E; D for APT28).
- **AI-written tool in a state destructive operation:** "The LazyWiper script was likely generated largely by an LLM and does not contain distinctive features." Its file-corruption method "was intended to be faster than overwriting the entire file; however, due to the way file operations are performed, it is in practice significantly slower." — [CERT Polska report](https://cert.pl/uploads/docs/CERT_Polska_Energy_Sector_Incident_Report_2025.pdf) (30 Jan 2026; P-full; M; D).

### Inferences
- **Success share for AI-orchestrated espionage:** about 3–5 of ~30, or 10–17%, if "a handful" means 3–5. That is my reading of a vague word. For context, Microsoft measured 29% intrusion success and about 7% confirmed exfiltration for Russia's human-run espionage outside Ukraine in 2022 (Q2). The two populations are not comparable, so this is no evidence that AI raises the success rate per target. It is evidence that AI raises *targets per operator*, roughly 30 at once.
- **Within correlation in the AI case:** GTG-1002 attacked each target with its own reconnaissance and exploits, which is the essay's "each hit in its own way". The outcomes were mostly failures with a few successes. No outcome per target was published, so ρ_w cannot be estimated. The design implies lower ρ_w than the single-access state operations in Q1, which supports letting ρ_w fall as the AI share of tactical work rises.
- **Noisy assessment:** AI overstatement ("credentials that didn't work") raises the noise in the attacker's own damage reports, as the model assumes.
- **Mapping to targets_end (default 30, range 1–300):** **supported** by GTG-1002 (~30 at once) and the August 2025 extortion case (≥17). Criminal mass campaigns (100 to 3,000 victims per flaw, Q4) show the upper range is reachable, but by shared-flaw campaigns, not by tailored ones.
- **Per-stage AI share:** human time per stage of minutes against hours of AI work fits the move of AI's tactical share from 10% to 85%. The human decision gates (4–6 per campaign) stay a fixed cost.

## 6. Conceptual and formal work on correlated outcomes (common-mode failure, salvo correlation, law-of-large-numbers arguments)

### Takeaway
Three formal tools fit the model. (1) Böhme & Kataria's twin-tier correlation, within a firm and across firms, maps onto within_correlation and between_correlation. (2) Reliability engineering's β-factor for common-cause failure, typically 1–10% in practice, translates into a pairwise correlation of about β. That supports a between-attack value of about 0.01–0.10 for attacks that share design factors. (3) Plain variance algebra reproduces the model's finding that doctrine forms only when between_correlation is below about 0.02. The limit is ρ_b ≤ 0.038·p/(1−p), which is 0.02 at p = 0.35, independent of fleet size. Practitioner evidence (Coveware) and analyst evidence (Bateman) go against a pure law-of-large-numbers "casino". Shared causes drift, and damage assessment is immature.

### Cited Findings
- **Twin-tier correlation, and the observed dominance of one common factor** — [Böhme & Kataria](http://www.econinfosec.org/archive/weis2006/docs/16.pdf) (2006; P-full; see Q3). They also list remedies: "software diversity to limit internal correlation"; global correlation "may be reduced by the maturing of those technologies, but it is unlikely to vanish completely". (P-full; O; T; pre-2020.)
- **β-factor range:** a search excerpt from an engineering safety document says "a method for evaluating of β is described in IEC 61508-6, Annex D. In practical use the value of β is normally in the range 1% to 10%." One project assessment there assumed a β of 20%. — [ESS probabilistic safety assessment](https://indico.ess.eu/event/1235/attachments/8929/13652/ESS-0052608_-_TSS_Probabilistic_Safety_Assessment_rev1ver32.pdf) (S-excerpt; I did not confirm which of the documents returned carries the quote; E; T). SINTEF's report of β-factors from operational data refused a plain request (403).
- **Battle damage assessment is immature:** "frameworks for assessing the battle damage of individual cyber operations remain immature"; a "cyber fog of war… continues to shroud even the most closely watched cyber incidents." — [Bateman, Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2022/12/russias-wartime-cyber-operations-in-ukraine-military-impacts-influences-and-implications) (16 Dec 2022; P-extract; O; D).
- **Practitioner view against "one flaw, many payments":** each successive mass campaign earned less (25% → 0% paying), and the per-campaign yield was unforeseeable — [Coveware, Feb 2026](https://coveware.com/2026/02/why-zero-day-downstream-mass-data-extortion-campaigns-are-losing-their-bite/) (P-extract; O/E; T).

### Inferences (independent mathematics; my derivations)
- **The doctrine threshold in closed form.** Take a campaign of A attacks with T targets each (N = A·T), planned success share p, within correlation ρ_w and between correlation ρ_b. The variance of the achieved share S is p(1−p)·[(1+(T−1)ρ_w)/N + ((A−1)/A)·ρ_b]. The criterion "P(|S/p − 1| ≤ 0.25) ≥ 0.8" (reading ±25% as relative to the planned share; normal approximation) needs a relative variance ≤ (0.25/1.2816)² = 0.038. As N grows, only the ρ_b term remains, so **doctrine needs ρ_b ≤ 0.038·p/(1−p)**: 0.013 at p = 0.25, **0.020 at p = 0.35**, 0.038 at p = 0.5 and 0.057 at p = 0.6. This reproduces the model's "only below about 0.02" without simulation if the planned share is about 0.35. It also shows the threshold **depends on p**: higher planned success makes doctrine easier. If the model reads ±25% as percentage points, the threshold would be far higher. The model's documentation should state which reading it uses.
- **Finite-campaign checks (p = 0.35, ρ_w = 0.3).** A one-month campaign of about 20 attacks with T = 30 targets each gives P = 0.85 at ρ_b = 0, 0.67 at 0.02 and 0.53 at 0.05. With T = 1 (today) and A = 20, P ≤ 0.59 even at ρ_b = 0, so doctrine cannot form today on sample size alone. A year of 235 attacks with T = 30 gives P = 0.79 at ρ_b = 0.02. With T = 30 the within term matters: at ρ_w = 0.9, P = 0.57 at ρ_b = 0.01 against 0.85 at ρ_w = 0.1 (A = 20).
- **β-factor as correlation:** if a shared cause fails a fraction β of all failures, the pairwise correlation of two components' failures is about β for small failure probability. My check: β = 0.01 gives 0.0099 and β = 0.1 gives 0.099 at q = 0.01. A transfer from safety engineering therefore gives **0.01–0.10** for attacks that share design or environment. This **supports** a between_correlation default of 0.05 and **narrows** the range upper bound of 0.3. The ±25% criterion needs < 0.02, which sits at the low end of what engineered redundant systems achieve.
- **Shared-stage formula for within correlation:** ρ_w = r(1−q)/(1−qr) (Q1). It links the owner's per-stage kill chain to the correlation. Stages that are shared across targets (access broker, management plane, tool build, C2) create ρ_w; stages done per target do not. For the surveillance chain the shared stages differ: collection is usually per target and the shared stage is reused tooling. The effects chain often uses one access or one command (Viasat, Stryker, Poland). So the two chains should get different ρ_w.
- **Avoid double counting:** the evidence in Q2 and Q4 shows most between-attack co-movement is drift caused by adaptation, which the model already builds as pooled alertness. If between_correlation also stands for that drift, the effect is counted twice. Proposal: define between_correlation as the residual shock per review period. Evidence: ≈0–0.003 per quarter (ransomware), ≈0.03 for arrival per day (honeynet, pre-2020), up to ≈0.05–0.08 a year when drift is included. Add rare common-mode jumps separately (CrowdStrike: 25% of large firms; WannaCry: 34% of trusts).
