# Workforce sizes and analyst work per operation: targeting, damage assessment, SIGINT language and processing, operators and researchers (US and allies)

Research date: 8 November 2026. Purpose: give SC45 (a country's cyber attacks against surveillance, 2026–2051) better anchors for its four expert groups and for analyst work per attack. Model defaults, from the task: a pool of 40,535 experts; researchers 3% (about 1,216); operators about 4,270 (2,482 attack, 1,790 surveillance implants); collection specialists about 32,200; analysts about 2,830 (1,044 attack planning and assessment, 1,790 surveillance access planning) at 90% use; about 326 attacks a year; 1.5 expert-years of planning and 1.7 of damage assessment per attack.

Conventions in these notes. "Quality" is one of: *official* (a government document or an on-record statement by an official), *official (leaked)* (Snowden or black-budget documents), *reported* (press with named or unnamed officials), *estimate* (a think tank or journalist estimate), *vendor claim*. "Read" says how I saw the text: *full* (I opened the page), *in an existing note* (another SC45 note read it; I did not re-read it), or *search summary only* (I did not open it; treat as unverified). "My calculation" marks arithmetic that no source states. Strategic, coarse figures only.

## 1. How many targeting analysts, analysts who support targeting, and battle damage assessment (BDA) analysts do US and allied commands have?

### Takeaway
No US or allied source gives a count of targeting or BDA analysts for cyber operations. The best proxy is the Cyber Mission Force's 25 support teams of 39 "linguists and analysts" (about 975 people, 2016–2018). That is 7% below the model's 1,044 attack analysts. In kinetic targeting, AI-enabled cells claim to cut staff or raise throughput by about 6× to 100× (2022–2026), but no source measures this for cyber targeting.

### Inferences
- **Attack analysts.** 25 support teams × 39 = 975 people (2016–2018), against the model's 1,044 (−7%). The fit is close, but it is not the same group. Support teams include linguists, which the model counts as collection. Mission teams and NSA or DIA analysts also do targeting work. So 975 is a floor for the CMF's own analysts and linguists, not a full count of attack planners.
- **After the 2028 growth.** The 14 new teams include at least 4 CSTs (Air Force 2, Navy 2; see section 3). Then the support teams have at least 29 × 39 = 1,131 people (my calculation). If the 4 Army teams include CSTs, the total can reach about 1,290. Against 1,044, that is +8% to +23% by Sep 2028.
- **AI uplift in targeting.** In kinetic targeting, sources claim about 6×–12× (IDF, 2022), 10×–50× (US, 2026) and about 100× (US exercise, 2024). These are throughput or staff claims for target nomination. They are not measurements of cyber target development. A cyber attack also needs network access and payload work. Cyber Command said in 2023 that the basic intelligence on networks "just wasn't there". Use these values as upper bounds on any AI multiplier for the planning share of analyst work.
- **Different regimes.** The IDF reported more than 12,000 targets in about one month with "hundreds" of staff. If "hundreds" means 200–500 people, that is about 0.4–0.9 person-days per target (my calculation). Some of those targets may come from a bank made before the war, so the true work per target can be higher. The model's 1.5 expert-years per planned attack is about 400–900 times more work. Mass kinetic targeting and tailored cyber attacks are different kinds of work, so do not transfer per-target effort between them.
- **BDA.** The only BDA evidence is qualitative. Glowing Symphony used collection assets for "indicators of success", and Cyber Command assessed it over up to 120 days. This supports two things: a damage assessment cost that falls partly on collection staff, and an assessment lead time of months.

### Gaps
- I found no count of targeting or BDA analysts at Cyber Command, the JFHQ-Cs, NSA, DIA, NGA or the services' targeting cells. Job adverts show such roles exist (for example "Joint Targeting Analyst" in the DoD Cyber Workforce Framework), but they give no numbers.
- No source confirms the size of a Combat Support Team. The 39-person value is assumed from the National Support Team.
- A search summary attributed the quote "There currently is no cyber analytic career field or even a career path for cyber analysts within the Department of Defense" to 2023 reporting. The article I opened did not contain it, so it is unverified.
- A search summary reported an NPR estimate (Dec 2023) that about 20 IDF intelligence officers produced 50–100 targets in 300 days before AI, and the Gospel produced about 200 in 10–12 days. I could not find or open the NPR article, so this is unverified. If confirmed, it would give about 60–120 officer-days per target without AI.
- I could not open Cyber Command's own CMF explainer (HTTP 403), Der Spiegel or The Guardian (fetch tool blocks them).
- I found no UK, Australian or Israeli count of targeting or BDA analysts.

## 2. How many SIGINT linguists, language analysts and processors exist (NSA, Army 35P, Air Force 1N3, Navy CTI), and what are the shortages?

### Takeaway
The only workforce-wide figure is from 2004: "over 6500" cryptologic language analysts worldwide. About 1,200 of them were NSA civilians, so about 5,300 (82%) were military. Qualified people worked only 51% of the more than 2,300 language missions. I found no 2015–2026 headcount for Army 35P, Air Force 1N3, Navy CTI or NSA civilian linguists. Older shortages are well documented: a 25% Army gap in Korean and Mandarin (2001), and fewer than half of linguists stay past their first tour.

### Cited Findings
- **More than 6,500 cryptologic language analysts worldwide; more than 2,300 language missions; qualified people on 51% of missions.** Population: the NSA/CSS cryptologic language workforce, military and civilian, worldwide. Date: 29 Jun 2004. Quotes: "over 6500 Cryptologic Language Analysts"; language missions "(2300+)"; "Currently 85% of the NSA/CSS cryptologic missions require a skill level of 3 or better"; "over 25% of current cryptologic missions requiring a level higher than 3"; "The overall worldwide LRI [Language Readiness Index] is 51%, with 80% representing a minimum acceptable risk"; "only half of the missions are being prosecuted by qualified personnel". Author: Renee Meyer, NSA/CSS Senior Language Authority. Quality: official (leaked; internal SIDtoday newsletter). Read: full. Maps to: the language part of the collection specialists, and their shortfall. — [The Intercept, SIDtoday, "Now You're Speaking My Language: NSA's Linguistic Resources (Part I)", 29 Jun 2004](https://theintercept.com/snowden-sidtoday/3233200-now-you-re-speaking-my-language-nsa-s-linguistic/)
- **About 1,200 civilian linguists; 75% of military and 49% of civilian language analysts below level 3/3; 600 more civilian posts planned over five years; 785 more military billets recommended.** Date: 16 Jul 2004. Quotes: "a current civilian cadre of nearly 1200"; "The 75% of the military CLAs and the 49% of the civilians who are not at level 3/3"; NSA "increased its civilian hiring ceiling by 600 linguists over the next five years", which "represents an overall net gain of 50%"; military language billets should rise "by 785 billets". Quality: official (leaked). Read: full. Maps to: the civilian and military split of the language workforce, and its growth. — [The Intercept, SIDtoday, Part III and Final, 16 Jul 2004](https://theintercept.com/snowden-sidtoday/3233206-now-you-re-speaking-my-language-nsa-s-linguistic/)
- **Army cryptologic linguists: 142 posts empty, a 25% shortfall in Korean and Mandarin (FY2001); fewer than half stay past their first tour.** Population: US Army cryptologic linguists. Date: report of 31 Jan 2002, data for fiscal year 2001. Quotes: "there were 142 unfilled positions, which amounted to a 25 percent shortfall" (Korean and Mandarin Chinese, two of six critical languages); "fewer than 50 percent stay beyond their initial 4 to 6 year tour"; "The Army noted that linguist shortfalls affect its readiness". Read: full text (the per-language table values were missing from the HTML). Date: 12 Jul 1994. Quality: official (GAO). Read: summary page only (the full text was not on the page). Maps to: the training pipeline is a limit on linguist supply. — [GAO/NSIAD-94-191, DOD Training: Many DOD Linguists Do Not Meet Minimum Proficiency Standards, 12 Jul 1994](https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi94191.htm)
- **Allied growth plans hire linguists.** Australia's REDSPICE (2022) lists linguists and analysts among its 1,900 new jobs (see section 1). Unit 8200's open-source unit, Hatzav, produces daily translations of the Arab press, and it is turning toward cyber work (ETH CSS, Dec 2019). Quality: reported; estimate. Read: full. — [CSO Online, 10 Apr 2022](https://www.csoonline.com/article/572527/how-asd-plans-on-spending-9-9-billion-on-cybersecurity-and-find-the-staff.html); [ETH Zurich CSS, Dec 2019](https://css.ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/gess/cis/center-for-securities-studies/pdfs/Cyber-Reports-2019-12-Unit-8200.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Military share.** 6,500 − 1,200 = about 5,300 military language analysts, or about 82% (my calculation, 2004). A search summary said "four-fifths" were military. I did not see that phrase in the parts I opened, but the arithmetic agrees.
- **Share of the collection group.** 6,500 is about 20% of the model's 32,200 collection specialists (my calculation). This holds only if the model's pool includes the service cryptologic linguists, and only at the 2004 level. Many of those linguists serve in service units and may not count among the NSA's 39,000.
- **Language capacity was the limit in 2004.** To raise the readiness index from 51% to the 80% minimum, qualified staff would have to rise by about 57% (80 ÷ 51 = 1.57; my calculation, if readiness scales with qualified staff). So processing demand was well above capacity. This supports a collection group with a backlog, and a large gain if AI translation substitutes for linguists. No source measures that gain for SIGINT.
- **Army posts in two languages.** 142 empty posts at a 25% shortfall means about 570 authorized posts in Korean and Mandarin (142 ÷ 0.25 = 568; my calculation, FY2001).
- **Turnover.** If more than half leave after a 4–6-year tour, the workforce turns over in roughly 5–8 years. Training must then replace about 12–20% of the stock each year (my rough calculation). So the supply of qualified linguists depends on a large and steady flow of trainees.

### Gaps
- I found no current (2015–2026) count of Army 35P, Air Force 1N3 or Navy CTI personnel, or of NSA civilian language analysts. I found no current readiness index or shortage figure.
- I found no separate count of "processors" (signals processing and exploitation staff). A search summary said the FY2013 black budget had 107,035 intelligence community positions, with 14,950 NSA military posts out of 23,400 military intelligence posts. I did not open a source for these numbers, and I found no split by function (collection, processing, analysis). — [Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/201407/500-billion-spent-on-intelligence-according-to-u-s-black-budget) (search summary only)
- A search summary gave a 1994 GAO figure of about 18,500 DoD intelligence linguists. The page I opened did not show it, so it is unverified.
- I found no reporting of cuts to linguist posts because of machine translation (searched for 2024–2026).

## 3. How many people does one offensive cyber operation involve, and for how long?

### Takeaway
Team sizes are public, but people or person-years per operation are not. The published sizes: NMT 64, National Support Team 39 and national CPT 39 (2016); a plan of 60-person NMTs and CMTs and 40-person CPTs (2015); a "68-person" CPT (2025). 14 more teams come by September 2028, 12 of them already set up by May 2025. Glowing Symphony (from November 2016) was "the largest and longest" US offensive cyber operation, with no public team size; its assessments ran up to 120 days.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **The model's attack split matches the team sizes.** Mission teams: 13 × 64 + 27 × (60 to 64) = 2,452–2,560 people, against the model's 2,482 attack operators. Support teams: 25 × 39 = 975, against the model's 1,044 attack analysts. Total attack side: 3,427–3,535 (my calculations; the CST size of 39 is assumed).
- **Attack side in 2028.** The 4 known new CMTs and 4 known new CSTs add 396–412 people. The 4 Army teams add 0–256, depending on their types. So the attack side reaches about 3,820–4,200 by September 2028, +8% to +19% (my calculation).
- **Work per attack, all attack staff.** 3,427–3,535 posts ÷ 231–326 attacks a year = 10.5–15.3 person-years per attack (my calculation). This assumes that all posts are filled and work only on attacks.
- **Analyst work per attack.** 975 support-team people ÷ 231–326 attacks = 3.0–4.2 person-years per attack (my calculation). The model's 1.5 + 1.7 = 3.2 expert-years per attack lies inside this range. This assumes that the support teams do only attack planning and assessment. They also do linguist work and support NMT missions, so the true analyst work per attack is lower, unless NSA and DIA analysts add to it.
- **A check on the model's 90% use.** 326 × 3.2 = 1,043 expert-years a year. That is about 100% of 1,044 attack analysts, not 90% (my calculation). Check whether the 90% applies to the attack analysts, to all 2,830 analysts, or to a different work rate.
- **Duration.** Large operations are campaigns of months. Glowing Symphony started in November 2016, and its assessments ran up to 120 days. The model's per-attack work is in expert-years. For a 64-person team, 1.5 expert-years of planning is about one week of the whole team, or several months of a small cell (my calculation). No source shows which pattern is normal.

## 4. How many offensive cyber operations does the US conduct per year?

### Takeaway
The only count of US offensive cyber operations is still 231 in 2011. The public counts after that cover defensive "hunt forward" missions: about 5 in 2018, 9 in 2021, 22 in 2023 and about 25 planned for 2024, and more than 60 in 28 countries from 2018 to September 2024. Cyber Command also ran "more than two dozen" operations against foreign threats to the 2020 election; the source does not say whether they were offensive or defensive. The UK NCF says it operates "daily" and gives no count.

### Cited Findings

Read: in an existing note (costs_ai.md). Maps to: the calibration of attacks per year. — [Washington Post via Atlantic Council, Aug 2013](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/natosource/us-spy-agencies-mounted-231-offensive-cyber-operations-in-2011-documents-show/)

Date: article of 4 May 2022. Quality: official statement, reported. ### Inferences
- **Hunt forward grew about 31% a year.** 5 in 2018 to 25 in 2024 is 5× in six years, so (25 ÷ 5)^(1/6) = 1.31 (my calculation). This is the tempo of defensive work by the national mission force. It is not a count of attacks.
- **The 2011 count came before the CMF.** In January 2014 about 2,000 CMF people were in place; by 2016–2018 there were about 6,200, of which about 3,500 were on the attack side (my calculation from team sizes). So today's attack-side staff is larger than in 2011. If operations grew in proportion to attack-side staff, today's count would be well above 231. The model's 326 (231 × 1.41) is a cautious increase. No source supports a specific value. Operations may also have become larger and longer, so the count may not grow with staff.
- **Election operations.** More than two dozen operations for one mission set in about one year is consistent with hundreds of operations a year across all mission sets. But the type of those operations is not known.

### Gaps

- A state-media report (Sputnik, March 2023) gives 47 missions in 22 countries on 70 networks. I did not use it. A Cyber Command release on Albania reportedly gives 44 deployments to 22 countries on nearly 70 networks, but cybercom.mil returned HTTP 403, so I did not verify it.
- I found no statistics on operations approved under NSPM-13.

## 5. How many researchers find and weaponize flaws in government and in contractors?

### Takeaway
No source counts vulnerability researchers in the US government or its contractors. In 2013 TAO had "over 1,000" staff, including "computer hardware and software designers, and electrical engineers", with no split by role. In Israel, the technology unit (Unit 81) is estimated at about 1,000 soldiers, about a fifth of Unit 8200 (2016 estimate). That is a much larger research and development (R&D) share than the model's 3%, but it covers all technology work, not only flaw research. In FY2013 the NSA spent $25.1 million on buying vulnerabilities.

### Cited Findings
- **TAO (2013): more than 1,000 people, including hardware and software designers and engineers; "the largest and arguably the most important component" of the NSA's Signals Intelligence Directorate.** The article also names smaller TAO units at NSA Hawaii, NSA Georgia, NSA Texas and Buckley Air Force Base. It quotes a former NSA official: by 2007, "600 intercept operators were secretly tapping into thousands of foreign computer systems". Date: 10 Jun 2013. Quality: reported (former officials). Read: full. Maps to: researchers and developers are part of the operator unit; operators were roughly half of TAO. — [Foreign Policy via Atlantic Council, 10 Jun 2013](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/?p=72649)
- **Israel: Unit 81, "AMAN's technology unit", has "around 1,000 soldiers, or about a fifth of Unit 8200's troops".** It researches and supplies technology (integrated hardware and software products). Date: Dec 2019 report, which cites Behar (2016). Quality: estimate. Read: full (PDF text extracted locally). Maps to: an allied R&D share near 20% in a SIGINT and cyber organization. — [ETH Zurich CSS, Sean Cordey, Dec 2019](https://css.ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/gess/cis/center-for-securities-studies/pdfs/Cyber-Reports-2019-12-Unit-8200.pdf)
- **NSA, FY2013: $25.1 million for "additional covert purchases of software vulnerabilities".** Quality: official (leaked). Read: in an existing note (costs_ai.md). Maps to: some research is bought from contractors and brokers instead of done in-house. — [Washington Post via Atlantic Council, Aug 2013](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/natosource/us-spy-agencies-mounted-231-offensive-cyber-operations-in-2011-documents-show/)
- **TAO in Texas: fewer than 60 specialists in 2008, with 270 planned for 2015.** Source: Der Spiegel (Dec 2013), as summarized by Wikipedia. Quality: official (leaked), reported. Read: search summary only (Der Spiegel blocks the fetch tool). Maps to: the growth of offensive staff, about 4.5× in seven years at one site (my calculation). — [Wikipedia, Tailored Access Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailored_Access_Operations)
- **Australia's REDSPICE hires engineers and data scientists as well as linguists and analysts.** See section 1. — [CSO Online, 10 Apr 2022](https://www.csoonline.com/article/572527/how-asd-plans-on-spending-9-9-billion-on-cybersecurity-and-find-the-staff.html)

### Inferences
- **The 3% research share is below the allied R&D share.** Unit 81 is about 20% of Unit 8200 (estimate). But Unit 81 builds general technology, and vulnerability research is a part of it. So the model's 3% (about 1,216) fits a narrow meaning of "researchers" (people who find and weaponize flaws). It is too low if "researchers" also includes tool and implant developers.
- **TAO composition.** 600 operators (2007) and more than 1,000 staff (2013) suggest that operators were about half of TAO. The other half were analysts, targeting specialists, developers and engineers. This is my reading of two figures from different years.
- **GENIE and TAO overlap.** GENIE's 1,870 staff (2011) is larger than TAO's "over 1,000" (2013). GENIE's count probably includes staff outside TAO (for example infrastructure or partner staff), or the TAO figure is a floor. The model takes its surveillance operators (1,790) from GENIE, so its operator count may include some developers and analysts.
- **Bought research.** At broker prices of $0.1–7 million per capability, $25.1 million bought roughly 4–250 capabilities a year (calculation in costs_ai.md). The researchers group should therefore include an outside supply, as well as in-house staff.

### Gaps
- I found no count of vulnerability researchers or exploit developers at NSA, CIA, the CMF, NCF, GCHQ or ASD.
- I found no headcount of the contractor ecosystem for offensive tools (exploit vendors and integrators), for the US or its allies.
- I could not verify the Texas TAO growth figures at the primary source.
