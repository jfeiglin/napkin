# Effects kill chain: stages, per-stage needs and success of state cyber effects operations

Research date: 2026-11-07. Scope: effects operations (attacks), not the surveillance chain. "The model" is the system-dynamics model of one capable state (calibrated to the US).

Tags after each finding:
- **Read**: `primary-full` = I read or text-searched the whole primary text; `primary-part` = only part of the primary; `abstract` = abstract or official summary only; `secondary` = a third-party report that I read; `search-summary` = only the search tool's summary of a page (weakest; quotes there are not checked against the page).
- **Kind**: `measurement` (counted or forensic record), `estimate` (an author's estimate), `opinion` (a judgment or assessment), `definition`.
- **Applies**: `direct` = a state effects operation (nearly all are Russian; transfer to the US force is across states); `direct-US` = a US operation; `transfer` = another field or actor class (for example a lab range or criminals); `analogy`.

## 1. What are the stages of an effects operation, and what does each stage need in people, time, testing and access?

### Takeaway
The standard models split an effects operation into an intrusion stage that is the same work as espionage (SANS Stage 1) and an attack stage (SANS Stage 2) that needs process-specific development, testing on similar equipment, and a trigger. Public sources give usable times per stage (months for access, two to eight weeks for the final weaponization and staging) but almost no people per stage; the only people figures are Stuxnet estimates (a core team of 5–10 for six months, without test, QA and management staff) and the AI Security Institute (AISI) estimate of about 20 expert-hours for a 32-step IT network takeover in a lab range.

### Cited Findings

**Stage models**
- S1. Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain (2011): seven steps — reconnaissance, weaponization, delivery, exploitation, installation, command and control, actions on objectives. SANS confirms the origin: "In 2011, Lockheed Martin analysts Eric M. Hutchins, Michael J. Cloppert and Rohan M. Amin created the Cyber Kill Chain™". — [Hutchins, Cloppert & Amin, LM white paper, 2011](https://www.lockheedmartin.com/content/dam/lockheed/data/corporate/documents/LM-White-Paper-Intel-Driven-Defense.pdf); [Assante & Lee, SANS, Oct 2015](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/White_Papers/SANS%20-%20ICS%20Cyber%20Kill%20Chain.pdf). [Read: LM paper not fetched in this pass (step names as widely cited; the Lockheed web page I fetched does not list them); SANS primary-full. Kind: definition. Applies: framework. OLDER]
- S2. SANS ICS Cyber Kill Chain (Michael J. Assante and Robert M. Lee, October 2015). Stage 1 ("Cyber Intrusion Preparation and Execution") has the phases Planning (reconnaissance), Preparation (weaponization or targeting), Cyber Intrusion (delivery, exploit, install or modify), Management and Enablement (command and control), and "Sustainment, Entrenchment, Development, and Execution" (act). Stage 2 ("ICS Attack Development and Execution") has Attack Development and Tuning, Validation (test) and ICS Attack (deliver, install or modify, execute). Quote: "The first stage of an ICS cyber attack is best categorized as the type of activity that would traditionally be classified as espionage or an intelligence operation." — [SANS, Oct 2015](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/White_Papers/SANS%20-%20ICS%20Cyber%20Kill%20Chain.pdf). [primary-full; definition; framework; OLDER]
- S3. Stage 2 needs development and testing, and can lag Stage 1: "It is in Stage 2 that the attacker must use the knowledge gained in Stage 1 to specifically develop and test a capability that can meaningfully attack the ICS." "There may also be significant lag between Stage 1 and Stage 2 operations due to the need for prolonged development and testing time." "the attacker must Test his or her capability on similar or identically configured systems if the capability is to have any meaningful and reliable impact." "for more significant impacts, significant testing may occur in which the adversary may acquire physical ICS equipment and software components." — [SANS, Oct 2015](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/White_Papers/SANS%20-%20ICS%20Cyber%20Kill%20Chain.pdf). [primary-full; opinion (expert framework); direct; OLDER]
- S4. The attacker trades effort, success and detection at each choice: "Cyber attackers decide what attack tool or method they will use against the target based on the trade-offs between effort required over some period of time, likelihood of technical success and risk of detection." — [SANS, Oct 2015](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/White_Papers/SANS%20-%20ICS%20Cyber%20Kill%20Chain.pdf). [primary-full; opinion; direct; OLDER]
- S5. Detection by stage: development happens mostly off the victim network, so "under normal conditions, the adversary's development and tuning is especially difficult to detect." Stage 1 itself can trip an effect: "due to sensitive equipment it is possible that Stage 1 adversary operations could lead to an unintended attack." Defenders can watch for "unusual acquisitions of such equipment that may indicate a Stage 2 attack". — [SANS, Oct 2015](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/White_Papers/SANS%20-%20ICS%20Cyber%20Kill%20Chain.pdf). [primary-full; opinion; direct; OLDER]
- S6. Difficulty rises with the designed effect: "a simple denial of service that disrupts the ICS is significantly easier to achieve than manipulating the process in a designed way or being able to attack the system and have the option of re-attacking". — [SANS, Oct 2015](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/White_Papers/SANS%20-%20ICS%20Cyber%20Kill%20Chain.pdf). [primary-full; opinion; direct; OLDER]
- S7. Access is usually kept for espionage and held for later effects: "In many cases, there is significantly more value, depending on the attacker's current goals, in performing espionage than in perpetrating an actual attack"; "Enjoying sustained access provides the opportunity for attackers to initiate follow-on actions later if they align with national security or military goals". Havex (Dragonfly cluster, at least three years of collection) was "a generally successful Stage 1 ICS attack. To date, there has been no documented evidence of Stage 2 activity." Stuxnet's intrusion collected data "over many years, so that the adversary knew the ICS and its network as well as, if not better, than the engineers and operators on site." — [SANS, Oct 2015](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/White_Papers/SANS%20-%20ICS%20Cyber%20Kill%20Chain.pdf). [primary-full; opinion; direct; OLDER]
- S8. MITRE ATT&CK for ICS (content version v20.1) has 12 tactics: Initial Access, Execution, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Evasion, Discovery, Lateral Movement, Collection, Command and Control, Inhibit Response Function, Impair Process Control, Impact. For example, Impair Process Control: "The adversary is trying to manipulate, disable, or damage physical control processes." — [MITRE ATT&CK for ICS tactics, v20.1](https://attack.mitre.org/tactics/ics/). [primary-full; definition; framework]

- People per stage: no public source gives them. The anchors are Stuxnet core development of 2.5–5 person-years (S9) or 20–30 person-years (S10), plus test-bed, QA and management staff, and AISI's ~20 expert-hours for a 32-step IT takeover (S16). The IT intrusion work is therefore tiny next to the model's 15 person-years per attack. Most of those 15 person-years must sit in target analysis, Stage 2 engineering and testing, keeping access alive, approvals and damage assessment.
- The SANS split (S2, S7) supports the model's choice to make the surveillance chain and the effects chain share Stage 1 and differ in Stage 2. In the public record Stage 1 often ends without any Stage 2 (Havex, S7; Volt Typhoon, S19).
- The war-start pattern (S22, S23) is direct evidence of a stock of advance access and prepared payloads: built over about 11 months, spent in about six weeks, then refilled at about one attack a week.
- Model-input mapping:
  - planning_share (0.3): S3, S13 and S18 point to a large non-AI share in OT effects (process knowledge, testing, approvals). In the one AI-run state campaign, humans did 10–20% of the work (A1), but that was IT espionage. Verdict: supports 0.3 as a middle value; the evidence suggests about 0.1–0.2 for IT-only effects and 0.3 or more for OT effects.
  - cost_now (15 person-years, 3–40): S9 and S10 put core development of the most complex known case at 2.5–30 person-years; native tools lower the cost (S15). Verdict: supports the range; the evidence cannot narrow it. 15 person-years is plausible for OT effects and probably high for IT wipers.
  - warning_time (0.1 years): the last steps took about 2–8 weeks (C3, C4, C8). Verdict: supports the order of magnitude.

### Gaps
- No public source gives staff or person-years per stage for any state effects operation. US Cyber Mission Force team sizes were outside this thread.
- No source gives a chance of detection per stage. Public cases are those that someone detected and disclosed, so they over-represent detection at or after the effect.
- I did not re-read the 2011 Lockheed Martin paper in this pass.

## 2. For documented operations, how long did each stage take, and at which stage did operations fail or get detected?

### Takeaway
In Russian effects operations from 2015 to 2025, access came about 2 to 11 months before the effect (median about 7–8 months); the final weaponization and staging took about 2–8 weeks. Stuxnet and TRITON took years, and Volt Typhoon has held access for at least five years without an effect. Where operations failed, they failed at the last stages (the trigger or the effect: Industroyer2, the 2016 protection attack, TRITON, the Polish heat plant), not at initial access.

### Cited Findings
- C1. Ukraine, 23 December 2015 (three distribution companies). Timeline: "The attackers appear to have gained access more than six months prior to December 23, 2015, when the power outage occurred"; "The entire attack from March 2015 – December 23, 2015". Execution: attacks "executed within 30 minutes of each other"; "the adversaries used the HMIs in the SCADA environment to open the breakers"; "At least 27 substations (the total number is probably higher) were taken offline across the three energy companies, impacting roughly 225,000 customers." At Kyivoblenergo "seven 110 kV and 23 35 kV substations were disconnected for three hours." Supporting attacks: KillDisk, malicious firmware on serial-to-ethernet devices ("blowing the bridges"), UPS changes and a telephone denial of service on the call center. Detection: "there did not appear to be any resident capability to continually monitor the ICS network". — [E-ISAC & SANS, 18 Mar 2016](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/Cyber_Events/E-ISAC%20-%20Analysis%20of%20the%20Cyber%20Attack%20on%20the%20Ukrainian%20Power%20Grid.pdf); CISA: "impacting approximately 225,000 customers", "probably following extensive reconnaissance of the victim networks" — [CISA IR-ALERT-H-16-056-01, 25 Feb 2016, revised 20 Jul 2021](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/ics-alerts/ir-alert-h-16-056-01). [primary-full; measurement; direct; OLDER]
- C2. Kyiv, 17 December 2016 (Pivnichna transmission substation, Industroyer/CrashOverride). Outage about one hour (about 75 minutes in some reports), about 200 MW, about one-fifth of Kyiv's consumption. — [LMU OT security notes](https://mniedermaier.github.io/OTsecNotes/413-industroyer.pdf); [Wikipedia, 2016 Kyiv cyberattack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Kyiv_cyberattack). Dragos's 2019 reassessment (Joe Slowik) argues that the attackers wanted to disable protection relays (a Siemens SIPROTEC denial of service) to cause damage when the line was re-energized, and "were hoping to cause more widespread outages and trigger a destructive event"; that part did not work. Reports also say researchers could not confirm that Industroyer directly caused the outage. — [SecurityWeek, 2019](https://www.securityweek.com/russian-hackers-behind-ukraine-power-outage-may-have-sought-more-damage). [search-summary and secondary; measurement (outage) and opinion (intent); direct; OLDER]. I found no dwell time for this case.
- C3. Industroyer2, April 2022 (a Ukrainian high-voltage substation operator). Initial compromise "no later than February 2022" (CERT-UA, as reported). ESET: compiled "2022-03-23"; destructive steps "scheduled for 2022-04-08", Industroyer2 run at "16:10:00 UTC"; "the attackers had prior control of the target's network beforehand"; ESET does "not know how attackers compromised the initial victim". Outcome: Bateman says the 8 April attempt was stopped in progress and no power disruption occurred (fetch-tool paraphrase). Conflict: CSO Online reports that an earlier CERT-UA TLP:AMBER alert to partners "suggested that at least two attacks were 'successful'". — [ESET, 12 Apr 2022](https://www.welivesecurity.com/2022/04/12/industroyer2-industroyer-reloaded/) [primary-full; measurement; direct]; [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/sandworm-hackers-fail-to-take-down-ukrainian-energy-provider/) and [CSO Online](https://www.csoonline.com/article/3656954/ukraine-energy-facility-hit-by-two-waves-of-cyberattacks-by-russia-s-sandworm-group.html) [search-summary]; [Bateman, 16 Dec 2022](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2022/12/russias-wartime-cyber-operations-in-ukraine-military-impacts-influences-and-implications?lang=en) [primary-part].
- C4. Sandworm, October 2022 (a Ukrainian power utility; Mandiant, 9 Nov 2023). "the intrusion began on, or prior to, June 2022 and culminated in two disruptive events on October 10 and 12, 2022." "the attacker potentially had access to the SCADA system for up to three months." "our analysis suggests the OT component of this attack may have been developed in as little as two months." "Sandworm potentially developed the disruptive capability as early as three weeks prior to the OT event, suggesting the attacker may have been waiting for a specific moment to deploy the capability." Effect: native MicroSCADA tooling likely tripped breakers, "causing an unplanned power outage that coincided with mass missile strikes on critical infrastructure across Ukraine." Two days later CaddyWiper ran, but "the wiper deployment was limited to the victim's IT environment and did not impact the hypervisor or the SCADA virtual machine." No outage length or customer count is given. — [Mandiant, 9 Nov 2023](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/sandworm-disrupts-power-ukraine-operational-technology/). [primary-full; measurement; direct]
- C5. Viasat KA-SAT, 24 February 2022. Access by "exploiting a misconfiguration in a VPN appliance to gain remote access"; "At approximately 0302 UTC on 24 February 2022, high volumes of focused, malicious traffic were detected"; "tens of thousands of modems that were previously online and active dropped off the network", including "several thousand customers located in Ukraine"; Viasat "has already shipped nearly 30,000 modems"; the network "was largely stabilized within hours and fully stabilized within several days"; modems "can be fully restored via a factory reset". The overview gives no date for first access. — [Viasat, 30 Mar 2022](https://www.viasat.com/perspectives/corporate/2022/ka-sat-network-cyber-attack-overview/). [primary-full; measurement; direct]
- C6. Kyivstar, December 2023 (Ukraine's largest mobile operator). The SBU's Illia Vitiuk: attackers were in the system "at least since May 2023", "probably attempted to penetrate Kyivstar in March or earlier", and had full access "probably at least since November"; they wiped "thousands of virtual servers and PCs" and "completely destroyed the core of a telecoms operator"; "about 26 million mobile customers" (other reports say 24 million). — [SecurityAffairs reporting Reuters, 5 Jan 2024](https://securityaffairs.com/156958/cyber-warfare-2/sandworm-inside-kyivstar-for-months.html). [secondary; measurement (official statement); direct]. An October 2023 CERT-UA report said Sandworm breached 11 Ukrainian telecom providers from May 2023 — [BleepingComputer, Jan 2024](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/russian-hackers-wiped-thousands-of-systems-in-kyivstar-attack/). [search-summary; direct]
- C7. Volt Typhoon (US infrastructure): at least five years of access with no effect triggered (see S19). [primary-part; measurement; direct]
- C8. Poland, 29 December 2025 (CERT Polska report, 30 Jan 2026). Targets: "at least 30 wind and solar farms", "a private company in the manufacturing sector", and "a combined heat and power (CHP) plant supplying heat to nearly half a million customers". Heat plant timeline: "the attacker's first activities took place between March and May of 2025"; "Between March and July 2025, suspicious activities were observed within the attacked organization's infrastructure, including reconnaissance, unauthorized access to data, and attempts to obtain user credentials"; "In late 2025, unauthorized activities associated with reconnaissance, credential theft, and data access were once again observed". Renewable sites: "In each affected facility, a FortiGate device was present, serving as both a VPN concentrator and a firewall"; on 8 December 2025 the attacker made configuration changes on an HMI computer at one site; "on 25 December 2025, at all facilities where the device was deployed, the attacker conducted network scanning and login attempts"; on 29 December the activity in individual substations "appears to have been at least partially automated"; the summary page says "The partially automated plan was triggered in the morning of 29 December." At the heat plant, access in late 2025 came through the SSL-VPN portal of a FortiGate device, with accounts "that were statically defined in the device configuration and did not have two‑factor authentication enabled". ## 3. What share of state effects operations met their intended effect, by class of target?

### Takeaway
At the strategic level, cyber incidents between rival states produced concessions in 2.8% of 433 incidents (2000–2020; down from 4.5% of 266 in the earlier version), and 1,841 cyber attacks in Ukraine and 682 in Syria had no measurable effect on fighting. In the 2022 war, about 50 Russian destructive cyber attacks in four months set against 3,654 missile strikes had a military effect only in one plausible case (Viasat). No public dataset gives success rates by target class; a tally of the documented cases shows that the payload often works, but the intended purpose is met in roughly one case in five and a lasting effect in almost none.

## 4. How do technical success (the payload ran) and the intended effect (the purpose achieved) differ?

### Takeaway
In every well-documented case, the payload ran but the defender restored service in hours to weeks: about 3–6 hours for the 2015 grid attack, about 1 hour in 2016, hours to several days for Viasat's network (about 30,000 replacement modems), next-day recovery for Ukrtelecom, and about 1,000 of 8,692 Stuxnet-era centrifuges replaced while uranium output rose. The intended purpose (military advantage, coercion, lasting loss of service) was rarely met, and in several cases even the facts of the outcome are still disputed, which supports noisy damage assessment.

- Effect duration against preparation time: in 2015, about 3–6 hours of outage followed about 9 months of preparation, so the effect lasted about 0.05–0.1% of the preparation time (my arithmetic). Even an outage of several days after at least 7 months of access, as at Kyivstar, would be a few percent at most. For the model, technical success and intended effect need separate rates; in these cases the ratio of purpose-level success to technical success is roughly 0.2–0.4.
- Noisy damage assessment: in at least four of the cases (Kyiv 2016 cause, Industroyer2 success, Viasat military effect, Stuxnet count) the outcome is still disputed in public analysis. US Cyber Command assessed its own 2016 operation (R10). This supports the model's assumption that lessons come from noisy assessments; the noise looks as large as the signal at the purpose level (inference).
- Defenders' recovery is the main reason for the gap (manual operation, backups, replacement hardware, EDR, alternative communications such as land lines and Starlink). This supports the model's link from exposed attacks to more alert defenders.

### Gaps
- I found no restoration time for Kyivstar or for the October 2022 outage in the sources I read.
- I found no quantitative estimate of Stuxnet's delay to Iran's program in a primary source in this pass.
- Predatory Sparrow cases (Iranian fuel stations 2021, Khuzestan steel 2022), a possible case of a state meeting a physical purpose, were not checked (search budget used up).

## 5. Which stages has AI already automated or shortened, with evidence, and how does that change per-stage time, cost and chance of success?

### Takeaway
By November 2026, public evidence shows AI doing 80–90% of the tactical work in one state espionage campaign (about 30 targets, 4–6 human decisions per campaign, success in a small number of cases) and completing a 32-step IT network takeover range on its own in 3 of 10 tries, but failing an OT range. No verified AI-run destructive or OT effects operation in the wild is reported: Microsoft says the most complex intrusions "remain manually driven", Google has not seen fully autonomous pipelines in the wild, and Dragos reports no adversary AI use in OT. AI so far shortens Stage 1 (reconnaissance, access, lateral movement, collection), not Stage 2 (process-specific payload development and testing), triggering or damage assessment.

### Cited Findings
- A1. AI did reconnaissance, found and tested vulnerabilities with its own exploit code, harvested credentials, extracted and sorted data, and wrote documentation; humans chose targets and built the framework. Speed: "thousands of requests, often multiple per second". ### Inferences
Which stages AI has changed (evidence to November 2026):

| Stage | Evidence of AI automation | Change in time | Change in chance of success |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target analysis and planning | Humans still choose targets (A1) | None shown | None shown |
| Reconnaissance | Yes, in a state campaign (A1) and in ranges (A2) | Hours (A2, A4) | — |
| Initial access | Yes: exploits and credentials (A1, A4); weaponization within a day (A6) | Months of access building could fall to hours or days in weakly defended IT (A2) | 3/10 for a full chain in a weakly defended range (A2); "a small number" of about 30 real targets (A1) |
| Persistence and lateral movement | Yes in ranges (A2); hand-offs in seconds (A5) | Shorter | Not measured against active defenders |
| Payload development and testing (OT) | No public evidence; the OT range was not solved (A2, A7) | None shown | None shown |
| Trigger and timing | Scripts and group policies, not shown to be AI (C8) | — | — |
| Damage assessment | Data triage and documentation by AI (A1), but no assessment of effects | — | — |

- **AI share of tactical work (10% today to 85%):** A1 already shows 80–90% in one state IT campaign in 2025, so the end value is reached for Stage 1 IT work, though not shown for Stage 2 OT work. The "today" value of 10% seems low for the most capable actors in 2026, but A6 says most complex intrusions remain manual, so 10% may still fit the average.
- **Targets per attack (1 to 30):** A1 shows about 30 targets per AI-run campaign (espionage), and C8 shows 30+ sites in one effects operation in 2025 without evidence of AI. The count of targets seems to depend more on whether targets share equipment and credentials than on AI alone (inference).
- **Time to build access (9 months to about a week):** the start value is supported (section 2). The end value is plausible for weakly defended IT (A2: about 20 human-hours; A6: weaponization within a day), but the 2025 real-world effects case still took months (C8), and no OT evidence exists.
- **Chance of attribution (70% to 40%):** every case in this file was attributed to a state, but the actor cluster was disputed (C8a). I found no rate. AI use adds a new place where activity can be seen, the model provider (A1 was found by the provider's monitoring) — an inference.
- **Share of attacks on access shared with surveillance (70% to 20%):** the SANS model makes Stage 1 espionage work (S2, S7); Kyivstar's access was dual-use (C6: the attackers could have stolen personal data, tracked phones and read SMS, per the SBU); the heat plant had data theft months before the wiper (C8); groups hand footholds to OT groups (S21); the Dragonfly cluster spied on energy in 2014 (S7) and is linked to destruction in 2025 (C8a). This supports a high shared share today, but I found no number.

### Gaps
- No verified public case of AI used in Stage 2 (OT payload development or testing), in triggering, or in damage assessment of an effects operation.
