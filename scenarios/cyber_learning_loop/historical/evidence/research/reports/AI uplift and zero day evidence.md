# Per-target AI shares decide SC45's attack volume

Public numbers can anchor or replace most of SC45's AI and zero-day inputs, but almost none measure the share of expert labor that AI does. Sources report output per person, speed, counts or shares of tasks, so every AI share below is a transfer or an assumption, not a measurement. The input that matters most belongs to the analysts. Kinetic targeting forces report AI gains of **about 6 to 100 times** in target output, speed or staff (2022–2026), but SC45 lets AI do only 20% of planning and 30% of damage assessment, even at the end of AI progress. AI also widens one attack from 1 to about 30 targets. The analysts' work per attack then rises from **3.2 to about 30 expert-years by 2051**, and from about 2030 the analysts, not the operators, limit how many attacks the force makes (my calculation from the model's cost formula; the scenario's [README](../../../README.md) reaches the same conclusion). Separate AI shares for per-target analysis (0.6–0.9 at the end) and for per-attack legal review and approval (0–0.2, set by policy) cut that work by 44–55% in 2051. The operators' end values already appear in single 2025–2026 cases, and frontier users already report the tenfold discovery gain that SC45 keeps for the end. For zero-days, the patch rate of 1.5 a year matches the 174-day median fix of known-exploited flaws, but the 14.5% yearly loss is RAND's all-route rate, not its 5.7% yearly rediscovery. Sharing and reuse rest on single cases, and at the defaults the force burns **about 170 capabilities a year**, more than the 63–106 zero-days that all actors are caught using worldwide each year.

## Analysts limit attacks once AI widens them to 30 targets

Each table in this report gives an input's value today, the evidence for it (value, setting, date, quality and source), a recommended value with its basis, and the mechanism that the evidence suggests. A basis is *reported* when a source gives the value, *transfer* when a value from another setting is applied here, and *assumption* when the scenario must choose. Recommended ranges are judgment; they are not measured intervals. "My calculation" marks arithmetic that no source states. The work and limit figures use the stage-cost formula of [the specification](../../../README.md#specification-when-a-countrys-cyber-attacks-start-to-pay) on the paths of the main fast-AI run: the fast path reaches two thirds of its end in 4 years, the slow path in 10. "Summary only" marks a source that the research saw only through a search summary.

SC45 prices each stage of an attack as hand work for the attack plus hand work for each target, scaled by AI's share and AI's overhead. The analysts do two stages: planning (target analysis, legal review and approval) and damage assessment. AI reaches both on the slow path. The operators' stages move faster, and the targets of one attack grow from 1 toward 30. So the analysts' work per attack rises from **3.2 expert-years today to 11.5 in 2030, 23.6 in 2035 and 29.9 in 2051**. The operators' work per attack rises from 7.6 to about 18, 25 and 23, before lessons and with the cost of attacks built in advance (my calculation). At today's split of the analysts, the attack analysts can plan and assess about 100 attacks a year in 2030 and **40–50 after 2035**, while the attack operators can build 100–140. The published runs agree: the fast-AI run makes **1,990 attacks in 25 years against 8,160 without AI** ([brief](../../../results/brief.md)).

Target analysis is where AI support is most advanced. In a 2024 exercise, about **20 soldiers of the XVIII Airborne Corps matched the output of a 2003 targeting cell of more than 2,000** ([Army Times](https://development.armytimes.com/?p=50904)). An NGA official credits computer vision with a tenfold gain and LLMs with a further fivefold gain in targeting speed ([CSIS, Jun 2026](https://www.csis.org/analysis/what-maven-smart-system-and-what-does-it-do)). US forces struck more than 13,000 targets in the first 38 days of the 2026 war with Iran, with Maven support ([Arms Control Today](https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2026-05/news/ai-plays-major-role-war-iran)). Israel's chief of staff said in 2022 that the IDF now produces a year's targets "in a month or two" ([JNS](https://www.jns.org/israel-news/idf-identifies-as-many-targets-in-a-month-as-it-did-in-a-year)). These multiples mix AI with digital workflow, policy and a tolerance of error, so they are upper bounds. Controlled studies give smaller gains: military intelligence students with LLM tools scored **59% higher** under time pressure, which equals AI doing about 37% of the work at fixed output ([Frontiers, May 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-dynamics/articles/10.3389/fhumd.2025.1540450/full)), and a task model before LLMs frees 17.5% of an all-source analyst's year ([Deloitte, 2019](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/insights/articles/2024/6306_future-of-intel-analysis/di-future-of-intel-analysis.pdf)).

Legal review and approval follow policy, not AI. A 2018 presidential finding cut CIA approvals of cyber covert action from "as much as a year or more" to "a matter of weeks" ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/cia-covert-action-and-operations-cyberspace)). Under the earlier directive, objections to GLOWING SYMPHONY needed weeks of NSC meetings ([National Security Archive](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/cyber-vault/2020-01-21/uscybercom-after-action-assessments-operation-glowing-symphony)). DoD policy keeps "appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force" ([DoD Directive 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.PDF)), and CENTCOM says that a human checks every step that involves AI ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/02/27/us_military_maven_ai_used/)). The counterexample is a warning about error. Israeli officers reportedly spent about 20 seconds on each AI-marked target, with about 10% misidentification; the IDF disputes the account ([+972 Magazine](https://www.972mag.com/lavender-ai-israeli-army-gaza/)).

Damage assessment splits the same way. First-phase checks of each target are fast and partly automated in kinetic war: Ukraine's DELTA system has verified **more than 130,000 destroyed or damaged targets** ([United24](https://united24media.com/latest-news/ai-powered-battle-network-ukraines-delta-fully-deployed-marking-130000-verified-russian-targets-12300)), and US commanders reportedly make new target lists "in a matter of minutes" after assessment ([Arms Control Today](https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2026-05/news/ai-plays-major-role-war-iran)). Assessment of a campaign's effect stays slow and human. Cyber Command's assessments of GLOWING SYMPHONY are dated **33 and 153 days** after its authorization, and they report difficulty in measuring the overall impact ([National Security Archive](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/cyber-vault/2020-01-21/uscybercom-after-action-assessments-operation-glowing-symphony)). A NATO paper says that cyber has no mature method of damage assessment ([NATO STO](https://review.sto.nato.int/images/Papers/2-Dondo-sas-spring-2025.pdf)). The closest AI figure is defensive: Cyber Command says AI cut its analysis of malicious traffic from "hours, days, or weeks" to "minutes and hours" ([CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/cyber-command-ai-gains-cybersecurity-network-monitoring/)). Assessment capacity has limited campaigns before: in Desert Storm, DIA assessed only 357 of 862 strategic targets ([GAO](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-NSIAD-97-134/html/GAOREPORTS-NSIAD-97-134.htm)).

The case for SC45's low shares deserves weight. The kinetic gains come mostly from imagery detection and data fusion, which have no public cyber equivalent; Cyber Command said in 2023 that its all-source intelligence on networks "just wasn't there" ([DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2023/02/28/cyber-command-working-to-create-an-intelligence-center/)). US policy keeps a human at the end of every chain. But these arguments bear on the per-attack work of review and approval, not on the repeated work for each target. The evidence therefore supports two AI shares for each analyst stage. Per-target analysis can move on the fast path to 0.6–0.9, while per-attack legal review and approval stays at 0–0.2 on a path that policy sets. Per-target damage checks can reach 0.5–0.7, and campaign-level assessment 0.2–0.3 on the slow path. With these shares the analysts' work per attack in 2051 falls from 29.9 to **13.4–16.6 expert-years**, so the analysts' limit on attacks rises 1.8–2.2 times (my calculation).

Three more mechanisms follow from the evidence. A cap near 0.9 on the per-target share, or an error cost above it, keeps the reported failure mode of fast human review out of the model. Approval time can be its own delay, which a change of authorities cuts in one step: at the end of AI progress an attack takes about a month to build (my calculation), so weeks of approval would nearly double its lead time. And damage assessment draws on collection, as GLOWING SYMPHONY's need for collection assets to gather "indicators of success" shows.

## Operators already reach SC45's end values in single frontier cases

The best-documented AI operations already sit near SC45's end values for access. Anthropic attributes a 2025 espionage campaign against about 30 targets to a Chinese state-sponsored group, and says AI did **80–90% of the campaign** with 4–6 critical human decisions ([Anthropic, Nov 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/disrupting-AI-espionage)). In July 2026 Sysdig described a ransomware operation that, in its assessment, an AI agent ran from initial access to encryption ([Sysdig](https://www.sysdig.com/blog/jadepuffer-agentic-ransomware-for-automated-database-extortion)). The defenders' view sets the limit. Microsoft says the most complex intrusions "remain manually driven in the majority of campaigns we observe" ([Help Net Security, Oct 2026](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/02/ai-cybersecurity-threats-microsoft-report/)), and the UK NCSC judges that human operators stay essential ([NCSC, Nov 2026](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/annual-review-2026/understanding-threat/ai-impact-cyber-threat)). So 25% today fits a force-wide average only if most teams do not yet run agentic tools, although 70% of bug-bounty researchers already use AI help ([HackerOne](https://www.hackerone.com/press-release/hackerone-report-finds-210-spike-ai-vulnerability-reports-amid-rise-ai-autonomy)). AI-run access to soft cloud targets takes hours ([Anthropic, Sep 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/threat-intelligence-report-september-2026)). No source times an AI against a hardened strategic target, so 0.55 years today stays an assumption.

Capability moves in months; adoption does not. The US CAISI measured **71% success for the best US model on 285 hard capture-the-flag tasks** in April 2026, and it puts the Chinese frontier about 8 months behind ([NIST CAISI](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/05/caisi-evaluation-deepseek-v4-pro)). The UK AISI estimates that the length of cyber tasks that models complete reliably doubles every 4.7 months, and a newer Mythos Preview checkpoint took over a 32-step range of about 20 expert-hours in 6 of 10 tries ([AISI](https://aisi.gov.uk/blog/how-fast-is-autonomous-ai-cyber-capability-advancing)). None of these ranges has active defenders, which AISI names as the next step for its tests ([AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities)). Field data lag the labs: the share of newly exploited flaws used on or before disclosure fell from 28.9% in 2025 to 23.4% in the first half of 2026, and AI-found flaws are exploited at the usual rate of about 1.3% ([VulnCheck](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/state-of-exploitation-1h-2026)). The only adoption number is a forecast. Experts put public authorization of autonomous cyber operations by NATO or a member at a median of **2041**, superforecasters at 2038 ([FRI](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/expert-and-superforecaster-forecasts)). SC45 runs access and execution on one fast path, which mixes capability with adoption.

Payloads are the operators' largest cost today: **4.0 of 7.6 expert-years per attack** (my calculation). The evidence splits by target class. An AI agent did the destructive step on IT databases in the 2026 ransomware case, but AISI recorded the first AI success on an industrial control range only in May 2026, in 3 of 10 tries ([AISI](https://aisi.gov.uk/blog/how-fast-is-autonomous-ai-cyber-capability-advancing)). At the frontier, working end-to-end exploits of a browser engine come in 12–14% of tries ([Anthropic, Sep 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/glm-5-3-and-the-spread-of-advanced-cyber-capabilities)). No forecast supports the 57% end share. It fits bespoke industrial effects on the slow path, while IT effects can move on the fast path.

AI work in SC45 costs supervision and compute, and both inputs sit above the evidence. The measured supervision ratios are below 0.2: 20 minutes of expert attention for 8 hours of model work on one exploit chain (0.04) ([Anthropic, Sep 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/glm-5-3-and-the-spread-of-advanced-cyber-capabilities)), and human steps of 2–20 minutes against 1–6 hours of AI work in the 2025 campaign (0.01–0.17) ([Anthropic report](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/ec212e6566a0d47/original/Disrupting-the-first-reported-AI-orchestrated-cyber-espionage-campaign.pdf)) (my calculations). Compute is the larger issue. At USD 0.4 million per expert-year of AI work against an expert's USD 0.42 million, **AI work today costs 1.15 expert-years for each expert-year that it does** (my calculation), so today's AI shares save nothing. 2026 cases cost far less: a Linux exploit chain for under USD 2,000 in under a day, where expert testers estimated weeks ([Anthropic, Apr 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/mythos-preview)), and working exploits for 428 of 841 recent known flaws at USD 2.77 each ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01835)). If those weeks are 2–6 expert-weeks, compute is about USD 0.02–0.05 million per expert-year of AI work (my calculation). A consequence matters for research: at today's overhead, today's AI shares barely move SC45, so the end values and their paths carry the results.

Flaw discovery shows the same gap between raw and usable output. Project Glasswing partners told Anthropic that their rate of finding flaws rose **more than tenfold** in the first month with Mythos Preview, and Mozilla found more than ten times as many Firefox flaws as with the previous model ([Anthropic, May 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/glasswing-initial-update)). Partners report at least 129,000 verified flaws in four months ([Anthropic, Oct 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)). But 90.6% of 1,752 assessed findings were real and only 62.4% held their claimed severity ([Anthropic, May 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/glasswing-initial-update)). The AI rated 91.5% of its findings critical or high where maintainers rated 51.3%, and 0.8% were fixed by September 2026 ([VulnCheck](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/anthropic-glasswing-receipts)). So SC45's tenfold end gain is already a present value for raw finds by frontier users; the gain on usable capabilities is lower, and human triage limits it.

## FBI audits give the collection queue its first source

The 2011 anchor for implant work holds, now in full text. The Washington Post article, republished by the Atlantic Council, says that GENIE's 1,870 staff "made full use of only **8,448 of the 68,975** machines with active implants" (12.2%), and that human operators were the limit ([Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/natosource/us-spy-agencies-mounted-231-offensive-cyber-operations-in-2011-documents-show/)). NSA documents of 2009 say "Human 'drivers' limit ability for large-scale exploitation", and the TURBINE system has controlled implants "by groups instead of individually" since at least 2010 ([The Intercept](https://theintercept.com/2014/03/12/nsa-plans-infect-millions-computers-malware/)). The republished text can move the scenario's claim on the 2011 program from unverified to verified. No source measures the share of implant work that automation does across a force. The scenario's 30% today sits at the low end of a 0.3–0.6 range, and the 2025 campaign supports 0.9 at the end.

For processing, translation studies give the closest numbers. Post-editing of machine output raised translators' output by 18% (phrase-based, literary text), 36% (neural, literary text) and 74% (software localization) ([Toral et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-humanities/articles/10.3389/fdigh.2018.00009/full); [Plitt and Masselot](https://www.academia.edu/85332400/A_Productivity_Test_of_Statistical_Machine_Translation_Post_Editing_in_a_Typical_Localisation_Context)). That equals AI doing **15–43% of the work** (my calculation). Commercial edit time per word fell from about 3 to about 2 seconds after 2017 ([Translated](https://translated.com/speed-to-singularity)), which implies up to about 70% for text in common languages (my rough conversion). Speech is harder: speech recognition helps transcribers only below about 30% word error ([Gaur et al.](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~fmetze/interACT/Publications_files/publications/asr_threshold_w4a.pdf)), and accented, tonal and low-resource speech lags behind ([FPRI, Jun 2026](https://www.fpri.org/article/2026/06/the-false-hope-of-ai-for-warzone-language-needs/)). More than 7,000 NSA analysts used generative AI within a year ([Defense One, Jul 2024](https://www.defenseone.com/defense-systems/2024/07/more-7000-nsa-workers-are-using-generative-ai-tools/398480/)), but no agency publishes a time saved. A range of 0.1–0.3 today and 0.5–0.85 at the end fits, with speech and low-resource languages slower.

Processing decides surveillance's gain from AI in SC45. At the end of AI progress, AI raises a specialist's reach in running implants about 5.1 times, but processing output only 2.0 times at the 0.6 end share (my calculation). The specialists process first, so processing takes most of their hours under AI, and its end share sets surveillance's gain. At an end share of 0.85, processing output rises 3.35 times. The input therefore also moves the leaders' split: a higher return of surveillance draws operators away from attacks.

The 0.1-year wait now has a source. In March 2005 the FBI's backlog of counterterrorism audio equaled **0.05 years (the FBI's refined count) to 0.21 years (all unreviewed audio)** of its yearly intake, and high-priority material had a 24-hour standard ([DOJ OIG, Jul 2005](https://oig.justice.gov/archives/reports/FBI/a0533/exec.htm)) (my calculation). An NSA official said in 2026 that current analysis "can take days or weeks", in the article's words ([Nextgov, Sep 2026](https://www.nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/09/nsa-looks-ai-help-analysts-sift-through-vast-data-troves/415835/)). Bulk buffers cap the wait at about 3 days for content and 30 days for metadata ([GCHQ document](https://assets.aclu.org/live/uploads/document/foia/GCHQ_Report_on_Capability_of_Tempora_Project.pdf)). The same audit shows loss, not only delay. About 27–29% of all foreign-language audio was never reviewed, and six of eight offices deleted unreviewed low-priority material. SC45 keeps all take in its queue until the specialists process it; a buffer that drops old take would match the evidence.

## Team arithmetic anchors the attack side; the agency side is a residual

The attack side matches public team sizes closely. Thirteen National Mission Teams of 64 and 27 Combat Mission Teams of 60–64 give **2,452–2,560 operators** against SC45's 2,482, and 25 support teams of 39 "linguists and analysts" give 975 against SC45's 1,044 attack analysts ([Federal Times, Dec 2016](https://www.federaltimes.com/home/2016/12/14/cybercom-evaluating-cyber-mission-force/); [Nextgov, Feb 2015](https://nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2015/02/need-job-cyber-command-halfway-full/104817)) (my calculation). Fourteen new teams are due by September 2028, and 12 existed in May 2025 ([DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2025/05/12/new-cyber-mission-force-teams-12-of-14-now-established/)). So the attack analysts could reach 1,131–1,290, 8–23% above today (my calculation). The support teams also hold linguists and serve other missions, while NSA and DIA analysts add targeting work, so 975 is a floor for the force's own analysts. The ratio of 975 support staff to 231–326 attacks a year gives 3.0–4.2 analyst-years per attack, around SC45's 3.2.

The surveillance side has weaker anchors. GENIE's 1,870 staff in 2011 set SC45's 1,790 surveillance operators. But TAO counted "over 1,000" people in 2013, including analysts, targeting specialists and developers, and a former official put its hands-on operators at about 600 ([Foreign Policy via Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/?p=72649)). No source counts the analysts who plan new accesses. The 32,213 collection specialists are the rest of NSA's roughly 37,000 staff after 2025 ([Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2025/12/nsa-has-met-2000-person-workforce-reduction-goal-people-familiar-say/409868)), so the group includes staff outside collection. In 2004 the NSA counted more than 6,500 cryptologic language analysts worldwide, about a fifth of SC45's group, with qualified people on only 51% of its language missions ([The Intercept, SIDtoday](https://theintercept.com/snowden-sidtoday/3233200-now-you-re-speaking-my-language-nsa-s-linguistic/)). The size of this group matters less than it seems: the model measures surveillance's value per surveillance expert of 2026, so the group's size changes the gain from AI, not today's split.

Researchers have no US count. Israel's technology unit is estimated at about a fifth of Unit 8200, but it builds general technology, not only tools that use flaws ([ETH Zurich CSS](https://css.ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/gess/cis/center-for-securities-studies/pdfs/Cyber-Reports-2019-12-Unit-8200.pdf)). NSA's FY2013 budget held USD 25.1 million for covert purchases of software vulnerabilities ([Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/natosource/us-spy-agencies-mounted-231-offensive-cyber-operations-in-2011-documents-show/)), so part of the supply comes from outside. At the defaults, SC45's 1,216 researchers find about 1,960 capabilities a year, 1.6 each (my calculation with the scenario's independent oracle, [tests/rest_point.py](../../../tests/rest_point.py)). The only count of attacks is still 231 offensive operations in 2011, before the Cyber Mission Force existed.

## Patch pace fits, rediscovery needs a new name, and burns overshoot

SC45 lets a known flaw or the target's own functions serve 80% of access and effect needs and 90% of movement and collection needs, on targets that have not fixed the flaw. At today's step times this gives one-day chances of 0.60, 0.77, 0.77 and 0.90, so an attack needs **0.86 zero-days** (my calculation with the scenario's oracle). By count, most exploitation uses known flaws: 71% of the 884 flaws first exploited in 2025 were exploited after their publication day ([VulnCheck](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/state-of-exploitation-2026)). High-profile flaws differ: 70% of Mandiant's 2023 set of exploited flaws were zero-days ([Mandiant](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/time-to-exploit-trends-2023)). Exploits started only 32% of the intrusions that Mandiant investigated in 2025, and phishing, earlier compromise and stolen credentials started most of the rest ([M-Trends 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/m-trends-2026)). In industrial cases, 73% involved stolen remote-access credentials ([Dragos](https://www.dragos.com/blog/dragos-2026-ot-cybersecurity-year-in-review)). Patches do not close those routes, so the share of needs that faces the patch race should be separate from the share that does not.

The fix rate of 1.5 a year has a direct source. Across 1.4 million organizations, the median time to fix a known-exploited flaw was **174 days, a rate of 1.45 a year**; flaws outside the CISA list took 621 days, a rate of 0.41 a year ([Bitsight, May 2024](https://www.bitsight.com/sites/default/files/2024-04/bitsight-a-global-view-of-cisa-kev-catalog.pdf)) (my calculation). About 72% of 2025's newly exploited flaws were not on the CISA list ([VulnCheck](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/state-of-exploitation-2026)) (my calculation), so a mixture of 0.4–1.5 a year fits all one-days better. Industrial systems fix slowly: 25% of 2025's industrial advisories had no patch or mitigation, and 71% of flaws wait for the next maintenance cycle ([Dragos](https://www.dragos.com/blog/dragos-2026-ot-cybersecurity-year-in-review)). That supports a never-fixed share of at least 0.25 and a slower pace for industrial targets; no source measures the inside of a network. One structural point matters more than the rate. SC45 opens the patch window at the start of each step, so when AI cuts steps to days, about 99% of targets are still open (my calculation). At the end of AI progress, the share of needs that known flaws can serve, not the speed of fixes, sets one-day use.

The 14.5% yearly loss equals RAND's mean life of **6.9 years** for a private stockpile of 207 exploits (2002–2016). That life ends by every route by which a flaw stops being private. RAND's own "likelihood of independent rediscovery" is **5.7% a year** ([Ablon and Bogart, 2017](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/us-17/wednesday/us-17-Ablon-Zero-Days-Thousands-Of-Nights-The-Life-And-Times-Of-Zero-Day-Vulnerabilities-And-Their-Exploits.pdf)). Bug-bounty data give 10.8–21.9% for heavily researched browser and mobile code ([Belfer Center](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/taking-stock-estimating-vulnerability-rediscovery)). A zero-day in use lasted 312 days on average before disclosure in 2008–2011 telemetry, a loss rate about eight times that of a held flaw ([Threatpost](https://threatpost.com/zero-day-attacks-thrive-months-disclosure-101612/77118)) (my calculation). SC45 already burns exposed capabilities separately, so the 6.9-year rate can count some losses twice. The input should be renamed as an all-route loss, or split into others' finds (0.04–0.06 a year) and other losses. AI raises finds faster than fixes: under 1% of 26,153 Glasswing findings were fixed by September 2026 ([VulnCheck](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/anthropic-glasswing-receipts)), so the defenders' AI gain of 2 can hold in the near term.

Sharing between espionage and attacks has one documented case. At least one of Stuxnet's four zero-days had served an espionage tool about two years earlier ([Kaspersky, Feb 2015](https://www.kaspersky.com/about/press-releases/equation-group-the-crown-creator-of-cyber-espionage)). The research found no public case of an unburned zero-day reused between two disruptive attacks, and documented disruptive attacks after 2016 mostly used known flaws or native functions. Reuse inside espionage is common: espionage actors used 83% of attributed in-the-wild zero-days in 2023 and 53% in 2024 ([Google, Mar 2024](https://blog.google/technology/safety-security/a-review-of-zero-day-in-the-wild-exploits-in-2023/); [GTIG](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2024-zero-day-trends)), and one exploit served two campaigns three months apart ([Google TAG, Aug 2024](https://blog.google/threat-analysis-group/state-backed-attackers-and-commercial-surveillance-vendors-repeatedly-use-the-same-exploits/)). So 10% and 5% stay assumptions, with the Stuxnet case as an upper bound near 25%. Surveillance's 95% reuse of its own capabilities agrees with long lives and low collision.

Burns on exposure are well supported. Every 2025 in-the-wild zero-day has a patch ([GTIG, Mar 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/)), and other actors pick up a patched flaw within days: five more groups exploited a 2021 mail-server flaw within 1–3 days of its patch ([ESET](https://www.welivesecurity.com/2021/03/10/exchange-servers-under-siege-10-apt-groups/)). The share of attacks exposed (0.8) and the accesses lost when a shared capability burns (0.02) have no public rate. A cross-check does exist. At the defaults the force burns **about 170 capabilities a year** (my calculation with the scenario's oracle). All actors together are caught using **63–106 zero-days a year** worldwide (2021–2025), and GTIG attributes 5–12 a year to Chinese espionage, the most active state ([GTIG, Mar 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/2025-zero-day-review/)). Defenders in adversary countries may not report what they find, so part of the gap is private burns. Even so, zero-days per attack, the exposure share or both look high for an average attack.

## Conclusion

The evidence turns SC45's caution around. The model is most conservative where public numbers are strongest, in the repeated per-target work of analysis and damage checks, and most generous where they are weakest: a fast path for autonomous execution that only one forecast addresses, and payload shares that no forecast supports. One AI share for each stage hides this, because AI acts on the per-target work while policy acts on the per-attack work. Because the analysts limit attacks, the unknown that matters most is not how well AI attacks networks, which benchmarks now track month by month. It is how much of cyber target analysis and damage assessment AI does, for which no public number exists. Coarse time records from one force's targeting and assessment cells would move this model more than any new benchmark of AI attack skill.

A second pattern runs through the evidence: SC45's anchors for today come from different eras. The share of implants used (2011), the queue (FBI, 2005), the loss rate of held flaws (2002–2016) and the count of attacks (2011) describe a force before AI, while the AI shares describe 2026. Where automation already acts, as in the group control of implants since 2010 or the triage of take, those older anchors overstate today's human limits. GTIG's yearly zero-day counts are the one current, systematic total against which the capability pools can be tested. Research that dates each anchor and replaces the oldest ones first would do more for the model than wider ranges.
