# Costs of state cyber operations and measured AI performance on offensive cyber tasks

These notes support the model of one capable country (calibrated to the US). The model splits a pool of cyber experts between surveillance (espionage) and effects operations (attacks), 2026–2051. Research date: 2026-11-07.

**How each finding is written.** Each finding gives the value and unit, what it covers (who, where, period, sample), a short quote, the source and its date, how I read it, the kind of number, and how directly it applies to a capable state's cyber force.
- **Read:** "primary, full" means that the fetch tool processed the whole page or document. "Primary, part" means only part of a long page. "Abstract" means the abstract page only. "Secondary" means a news or other report about the source. "Search summary" means that I saw only a search-engine summary and did not read the page. Treat search summaries with low confidence.
- **Kind:** measurement, author's estimate, plan or statute, or opinion.
- **Applies:** "direct" (data about a state cyber force), "transfer" (measured in another setting: a benchmark, a test range, commercial testing, or another actor), or "analogy".
- The fetch tool extracted the quotes from the page text. Spot-check a quote against its page before you publish it.
- Data older than 2020 carry the label "older than 2020".
- Money is in nominal US dollars of the stated year. My conversions to 2025 dollars use a CPI-U factor of about 1.38 from 2013 to 2025 (2013 annual average 232.957; 2025 annual average about 322). I did not verify the 2025 value in this session.
- The web search budget, which all agents in this session share, ran out part way through the work. After that, I fetched only known URLs. Some gaps come from this limit.

## 1. People and money: force structure, operations per year, budgets, the 2025 law and the cost of one expert-year

### Takeaway
The only official count of US offensive cyber operations is 231 in 2011, from the leaked FY2013 intelligence budget; I found no later count. The default of about 15 person-years per attack divides a 2016–2018 force structure (3,535 attack-oriented posts) by that 2011 count, while the 2011 implant program (GENIE) had 1,870 staff, which gives about 8 person-years per operation. Budget, statute and pay data support the default expert cost (about $0.42 million per expert-year, fully loaded) and the $1 billion surge. A 2025 NSA cut of about 2,000 people makes the default pool about 5% too high.

### Inferences
- **The default of 15 person-years per attack joins numbers from two periods.** 3,535 ÷ 231 = 15.3 person-years per operation. The 3,535 posts are CMF posts of 2016–2018. CMF teams did not exist in 2011, and "U.S. intelligence services" made the 231 operations.
- **Team sizes give the 3,535 and 6,187 figures exactly.** 13 NMT × 64 + 27 CMT × 64 + 25 support teams × 39 = 832 + 1,728 + 975 = 3,535. Add 68 CPT × 39 = 2,652, and the total is 6,187. The sources I read confirm only the sizes 64 (NMT) and 39 (National Support Team, national CPT). A CMT size of 64 and a Combat Support Team size of 39 are assumptions of this calculation.
- **The 2011 program staff suggest a lower cost per operation.** GENIE had 1,870 staff, and 1,870 ÷ 231 = 8.1 person-years per operation if all of them worked on the 231 operations. GENIE also ran espionage implants on tens of thousands of machines, so its attack share was smaller. Other NSA and military staff also supported operations. So 8 is a rough central value with wide uncertainty, not a bound.
- **In 2013 the offensive unit cost about $0.35 million per person, fully loaded.** $651.7 million ÷ 1,870 staff = $0.348 million per person-year in 2013 dollars. This includes infrastructure and the $25.1 million of exploit purchases. At a CPI factor of about 1.38, it is about $0.48 million in 2025 dollars.
- **Salaries alone are below the model's range.** GS-13 to GS-15 salaries ($0.12–$0.20 million) and the pentester salary ($0.125 million) are below the $0.25 million floor. With benefits, overhead, tools and infrastructure, a fully loaded cost of $0.3–$0.5 million is plausible. The FY2026 CMF figure gives $1.3 billion ÷ 6,187 people = $0.21 million per person. The CRS text does not say whether that figure includes military pay, which the services usually fund; I did not verify this.
- **The NSA civilian workforce may be about 19,000–25,000.** If 1,500–2,000 positions are 8% of the civilian workforce, that workforce is 18,750–25,000 people. Then about 14,000–20,000 of the 39,000 would be military. This is my calculation from reported numbers, and it is uncertain.
- **The 2025 NSA cut lowers the pool.** 39,000 − 2,000 + 3,535 = 40,535, about 5% below the default of 42,535. Not all NSA employees are cyber experts (there are linguists, analysts and support staff), so the pool that can work on operations may be smaller still. Some military members at the NSA may also fill CMF posts; I found no data on this overlap.
- **The surge funding is small for the pool but large for attack posts.** $0.25 billion a year ÷ $0.42 million = about 595 expert-years a year. That is +1.4% of the pool of 42,535, but +17% of the 3,535 attack posts. $1 billion is 38% of the FY2026 Cyber Command resources ($2.6 billion), and $0.25 billion a year is about 19% of the $1.3 billion CMF figure. The statute does not phase the money by year and does not limit it to people; it can also buy tools, access or contractors.
- **The $250 million for Cyber Command AI is a direct US input to automation.** It shows that the US funds AI for its cyber force from 2025. This bears on how fast the model's automation share rises in the US.

## 2. Tool and access costs: exploit prices, costs of major operations and the cost of keeping access

### Takeaway
Broker price lists put a full exploit chain for a phone at $5–20 million and an exploit for a network edge device at $50,000–$100,000. In FY2013 the NSA spent $25.1 million on vulnerability purchases. Estimates of Stuxnet's cost run from a few million dollars for the code to about $1 billion for the whole operation, so the cost of attacks has a heavy tail. Access can last years (Volt Typhoon kept some access for at least five years), and in 2011 the NSA fully used only about 12% of the machines that it had implanted; I found no measured cost of keeping access alive.

### Inferences
- **One bought access tool costs 0.1–50 expert-years.** At $0.42 million per expert-year, a $100,000 edge-device exploit equals 0.24 expert-years, a $5–7 million iOS chain equals 12–17 expert-years, and a $20 million offer equals 48 expert-years. For phone targets, the tool cost of one attack can be larger than its labor cost; for most network targets it is not.
- **The 2013 purchase budget bought roughly 4–250 capabilities a year.** $25.1 million ÷ $0.1–7 million each (my calculation; it uses current prices for a 2013 budget).
- **The cost of attacks has a heavy tail.** If the whole Stuxnet operation cost about $1 billion, it equals about 2,400 expert-years at $0.42 million (my calculation). The code-only estimate (10 programmers for six months, or 5 person-years) falls inside the model's range of 3–40. The range describes a typical operation, not a flagship operation.
- **Access is cheap to hold and costly to use.** In 2011, GENIE fully used about 12% of its implanted machines (8,448 ÷ 68,975). Human attention, not the number of implants, limited use. TURBINE shows that the NSA had automated part of implant management by 2013, before AI agents existed.
- **Pre-positioned access can last years.** Volt Typhoon's five years, and VulnCheck's finding that 36% of the flaws first exploited in 2025 were published 1 year or more earlier, both show that access can last years when defenders do not patch. So the waiting cost of advance access is mainly the labor to keep it hidden and current, not a repeated purchase.

## 3. AI on offensive tasks, by stage: success, time, cost and rate of change

### Takeaway
Frontier AI already does most of the tactical work in some real state operations: in September 2025 a group that Anthropic assesses as Chinese state-sponsored used AI for 80–90% of an espionage campaign, with humans at 4–6 decision points. Anthropic's September 2026 report says that most of the operations it disrupted were AI-enabled. Measured capability rises fast: the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) cut its estimate of the doubling time of cyber task length from 8 to 4.7 months, and in April–May 2026 one model completed a 32-step network attack range (about 20 expert hours) in 3 to 6 of 10 attempts. Cost per task is low ($3–$150 for a single flaw; under $2,000 for a kernel exploit chain). Reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, exploitation and movement inside a network are the most automated stages; target selection, effects on industrial systems, triggering and damage assessment are the least automated or are not measured.

- **The 85% end value is already observed today in espionage.** The 80–90% figure comes from one espionage campaign in September 2025. So it describes the present for a frontier user, not the end of AI's progress. It is a share of campaign activity as Anthropic counted it, not of human labor time; the human work to build the attack framework does not appear in it.
- **The effects chain has much less evidence than the surveillance chain.** The real-world cases are espionage, theft and extortion. They end with data taken and analyzed, which AI does well. Effects operations add payloads for specific target systems (often industrial), timing, control of side effects and damage assessment. AISI's industrial range is the only measurement, and the first success came in May 2026. To apply 85% to effects operations is a transfer from the surveillance chain.
- **Human share in the observed espionage campaign was 10–20%.** If AI did 80–90% of the campaign, humans did 10–20%, including target selection and 4–6 decisions. With the model's defaults (planning share 0.3, AI doing 85% of tactical work), the human share would be 0.3 + 0.7 × 0.15 = 0.41, about twice the observed value. For the surveillance chain, a planning share of 0.1–0.2 fits better. For effects operations, the NCF statements and the need for legal review, targeting and damage assessment support a higher share (0.3–0.6), but I found no number.
- **Success rates are low in real operations.** The 2025 campaign succeeded in "a small number" of about 30 targets; the 2026 hacktivist campaign got inside 14 of 42 targets; one 2026 actor never gained access. Ranges give 30–60% full success without defenders. More attempts per success may be needed than the person-year arithmetic shows.
- **Capability rises faster than the trend fits.** The 80% task length went from 16 minutes (Claude Sonnet 4.5, late 2025) to at least 8 hours (newer Mythos Preview checkpoint, May 2026): at least 30 times, or about 5 doublings in about 7–8 months, or about one doubling every 1.5 months (my calculation; AISI declines to call it a new trend). Claude Sonnet 4.5 may not have been the most capable model in late 2025, so this calculation may overstate the rate. Firefox exploits went from 2 to 181 in about 2 months; AIxCC discovery from 37% to 86% in one year; OpenAI's capture-the-flag score from 27% to 76% in about 3 months (search summary only).
- **Cost per task is far below the cost of labor for single tasks.** A fully loaded expert hour costs about $233 ($0.42 million ÷ 1,800 working hours; the hours are my assumption). Agents cost $18–59 per hour (ARTEMIS), $2.77–8.80 per known-flaw exploit, $24 per zero-day web exploit and $152 per discovery-and-patch task. A Mythos Preview exploit chain cost under $2,000 where experts estimated weeks of work, which is about $19,000–28,000 at $233 per hour for 80–120 hours (my calculation).
- **For long autonomous runs at frontier prices, compute cost is near labor cost.** A range attempt with the full 100 million token budget would cost $2,500–12,500 at $25–125 per million tokens (my calculation; AISI does not say how many tokens a run used). That is the same order as 20 expert hours ($4,700 at $233 per hour), with a 30–60% success rate. The model does not appear to count compute cost; at 2026 prices this omission is small for short tasks but not for long autonomous operations.
- **The most capable models are not freely available.** Anthropic limits Mythos Preview to selected partners. A capable state may get access through partners or build its own; a less capable state may not. This bears on the US advantage that the model assumes.

## 4. Time-to-exploit trends

### Takeaway
Mandiant's average time to exploit fell from 63 days (2018–2019) to 5 days (2023), before AI agents were in use. VulnCheck's share of newly exploited flaws that attackers used on or before the day of CVE publication rose from 23.6% (2024) to 28.96% (2025), and VulnCheck calls the timing "highly consistent" between the two years. These field measurements of all attackers do not yet show a sharp change from AI in 2025, although lab capability rose fast in the same period.

### Cited Findings
- **Mandiant time to exploit: 63 days (2018–2019), 44 days (2020 to the start of 2021), 32 days (2021–2022), 5 days (2023).** Quotes: "we observed an average TTE of 63 days"; "that number decreased to 44 days"; "the average observed TTE dropped further to 32 days"; "an average of just five days". Sample: "Mandiant analyzed 138 vulnerabilities that were disclosed in 2023 and that we tracked as exploited in the wild"; "the majority (97) of these vulnerabilities were exploited as zero-days" (n-day to zero-day ratio 30:70 in 2023). Definition: "Time-to-exploit (TTE) is our metric for defining the average time taken to exploit a vulnerability before or after a patch is released." — [Google Cloud, Mandiant, Casey Charrier and Robert Weiner](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/time-to-exploit-trends-2023), 15 Oct 2024. Read: primary, full. Kind: measurement. Applies: direct for attackers in the wild (state and criminal). The 2018–2019 values are older than 2020. Some secondary reports put the 44 days in "2020–2021"; the primary gives "2020 to start of 2021".
- **VulnCheck: 28.96% of 884 newly exploited flaws in 2025 were exploited on or before the day of CVE publication, against 23.6% in 2024.** Quotes: "VulnCheck identified 884 Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEVs) for which evidence of exploitation was observed for the first time"; "28.96% of KEVs in 2025 were exploited on or before the day their CVE was published, an increase from the 23.6% observed in our 2024 trends in exploitation report"; "Time to exploitation remained highly consistent between 2024 and 2025"; "Not all KEVs being exploited on the same day of CVE issuance are Zero Days." — [VulnCheck, State of Exploitation 2026, Patrick Garrity](https://wwv.vulncheck.com/hubfs/Research/2026-StateofExploitation.pdf?hsLang=en), PDF created 9 Feb 2026. Read: primary, full (PDF text extracted). Kind: measurement. Applies: direct for attackers in the wild. The report gives no split between state and criminal attackers.
- **VulnCheck first half of 2025: 32.1% of 432 flaws.** — [VulnCheck, State of Exploitation 1H 2025](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/state-of-exploitation-1h-2025). Read: search summary only. The full-year value (28.96%) is lower than the half-year value.
- **Google tracked 75 zero-days exploited in 2024, against 98 in 2023 and 63 in 2022.** — [The Record](https://therecord.media/google-zero-day-report-2024), April 2025. Read: search summary only. Kind: measurement.

### Inferences
- **The time to exploit fell sharply before AI agents.** The fall from 63 days to 5 days (2018–2023) came from a larger share of zero-days and faster n-day exploitation, not from AI agents.
- **The 2025 field data do not yet show AI.** The share exploited on or before disclosure rose by about 5 points (23.6% to 28.96%), and VulnCheck calls the timing consistent. Lab capability (section 3) rose much faster. This gap between lab capability and field speed supports a slower diffusion of AI into real operations than capability trends alone suggest. The field data may also lag, because a capable state hides its best capabilities.
- **Patch windows for known flaws are short.** For access by n-day exploitation, attackers must act or make access persistent within days. For older, unpatched systems (36% of 2025's newly exploited flaws were 1 year old or more), access windows last years.
